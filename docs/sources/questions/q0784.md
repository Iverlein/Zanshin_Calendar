# Written sources on reading dreams, and whether any ties them to the calendar

Are there written sources on reading dreams (རྨི་ལམ་བརྟག་པ, rmi lam brtag pa; the dream signs, རྨི་ལྟས, rmi ltas), in the Tibetan tradition and in the Japanese almanac tradition, and does any of them tie the dream to the calendar: to the lunar day, the weekday, the month, the year or the hour? Asked by the owner on 2026-11-09.

## Answered 2026-11-09

Written sources exist on both sides. The only time rule found is the watch of the night in which the dream came; no source found reads a dream by the lunar day, the weekday, the month or the year. In Japan a dream enters the calendar at one point, the first dream of the year (初夢, hatsuyume), a dated observance, not an annotation of the day.

**What the pool already holds.** The White Beryl reads dreams as signs observed, never by date: the dreams before the asking or the reckoning (ch. 30, wb.2:p144, built as the family `MomentSign.DREAM`; q348 settled the word «རྨི་ལྟས» on the scan); dreams as symptoms of the sick by birth animal and by *sme ba* (ch. 25, [corpus/wb/ch25.md](../corpus/wb/ch25.md), «ཕོ་མཆིན་མི་བདེ་རྨི་ལམ་རྩུབ» for the ox, the shapes of bad dreams poured into a skull in the dragon year's ransom); dreams in the readings of the sixty years (ch. 18) and the turtle classes (ch. 27); the dreams each trigram's year of life brings ([year-of-life.md](../year-of-life.md), pp. 391–396); «དེ་རྗེས་གོང་ལྟར་རྨི་ལྟས་བརྟག», "then examine the dream signs as above", before the grave ground is founded (ch. 32, [corpus/wb/ch32.md](../corpus/wb/ch32.md)); and the digest of p. 474, where the clan's token animal is found "from family tradition and dreams" ([personal-mansions.md](../personal-mansions.md)).

**The Four Tantras** (རྒྱུད་བཞི, rgyud bzhi; above WB on medicine), read on 2026-11-09 in BDRC's etext of the Gser thang edition (MW3CN5036, IE0OPIFEF6EACA, etext page = image, not read on the scan). The Explanatory Tantra (བཤད་རྒྱུད, bshad rgyud), ch. 7, *the signs of the body's destruction* (ལུས་ཀྱི་འཇིག་ལྟས, lus kyi 'jig ltas), etext p. 44: «རྨི་ལམ་རྣམ་འགྱུར་རིང་དང་མ་ངེས་ལྟས། །ཚོགས་གསོག་ཀློག་སྒྲུབ་ཡི་དམ་སུམ་འགྱུར་བཟློག །ཉེ་དང་ངེས་པའི་ལྟས་ལ་འཆི་བསླུ་བྱ། །ཤིན་ཏུ་ཉེ་བའི་ལྟས་ལ་ཟློག་ཐབས་མེད།», dreams and changes of bearing are the distant and uncertain signs of death, turned back by gathering merit, recitation and the yidam's practice; the near and certain signs call for the death ransom (འཆི་བསླུ, 'chi bslu); the very near ones nothing turns. Dreams are diagnostic elsewhere too: in pregnancy a dream in which the body is light and a man is seen foretells a boy (p. 34); fevers bring many confused dreams (pp. 202, 225, 260); a dream of water, trees, meadows, frogs, fish and insects on the body marks an illness sent by the nāgas (ཀླུ, klu; pp. 362–363); a dream of being led away by women marks a harming spirit (གདོན, gdon; p. 507). None is tied to a date.

**The Tengyur.** *The examination of dreams* (རྨི་ལམ་བརྟག་པ, rmi lam brtag pa; Sanskrit *Svapnohana*, «སྭབྣཱ་ཨུ་ཧཱ་ན» as the Derge print spells it) by the eastern Indian paṇḍita Vibhūticandra (བི་བྷཱུ་ཏི་ཙནྡྲ, bi bhU ti tsandra), who translated it himself; Derge Tengyur, BDRC MW23703_1749, vol. 28, p. 268 l. 4 to p. 271 l. 4, read whole in the etext (VE23703_1344) on 2026-11-09. Its opening is the one time rule found anywhere: «དང་པོའི་ཐུན་ལ་རྨི་ལམ་བྱུང་། །ལོ་གཅིག་ནང་དུ་འབྲས་བུ་འབྱིན། །ཐུན་གཉིས་ཟླ་བ་བརྒྱད་བར་ལ། །ཐུན་གསུམ་ཟླ་བ་གསུམ་གྱི་བར། །ཐུན་བཞི་ཟླ་བ་ཕྱེད་བར་དུ། །མཐོང་བར་འགྱུར་བ་ཐེ་ཚོམ་མེད། །སྐྱ་རེངས་ཤར་བའི་དུས་ཉིད་ལ། །ཞག་བཅུའི་ནང་དུ་འབྲས་བུ་འབྱིན།», a dream in the first watch (ཐུན, thun) of the night bears fruit within a year, in the second within eight months, in the third within three months, in the fourth within half a month, and a dream at the rising of dawn within ten days. Then a catalogue of dream images with what each brings (riding an ox or an elephant, a mansion, a hill top, a tree in fruit, a boat; the sun's or the moon's disc dreamt frees the sick of illness and brings the well to glory; a white snake seizing the right hand, wealth within ten; a woman in red embraced, no next night; eating human flesh, a kingdom), and a remedy for bad dreams: a fire offering of red sandalwood smeared with butter and a thousand Gāyatrī, and «གང་གིས་ནང་པར་ཀློག་པ་དེའི། །རྨི་ལམ་ངན་པ་ཟློག་པར་འགྱུར», whoever reads the text in the morning turns his bad dreams back. The watches are the night's own divisions; no lunar day, weekday or month is named. A second *rmi lam brtag pa* by the Indian master Gnyis su med pa (གཉིས་སུ་མེད་པ, Advayavajra), in the Mañjuśrī cycle, Derge Tengyur vol. 49 pp. 261–262 (MW23703_2100, etext VE23703_1365), was located and not read. The modern study is Rolf Scheuermann, *Vibhūticandra's Svapnohana and the Examination of Dreams*, in *Glimpses of Tibetan Divination: Past and Present*, eds. Petra Maurer, Donatella Rossi and Rolf Scheuermann, Brill 2020 (Prognostication in History 2), pp. 161–180 (DOI 10.1163/9789004410688_009), not read (Brill and OAPEN refuse automated browsers; PLAN.md, *Dead ends*).

**Other Tibetan dream manuals, found by title on BDRC and not read** (2026-11-09; the search «རྨི་ལམ་བརྟག» gives 863 hits, «རྨི་ལྟས» 2172): *The stages of the signs of good and bad dreams* (རྨི་ལམ་བཟང་ངན་བརྡ་རྟགས་ཀྱི་རིམ་པ, rmi lam bzang ngan brda rtags kyi rim pa) from the *Lama Gongdü* (བླ་མ་དགོངས་འདུས, bla ma dgongs 'dus) treasure, in the *Rinchen Terdzö* and many prints; *The magic mirror, a method of examining dreams* (རྨི་ལམ་བརྟག་ཐབས་འཕྲུལ་གྱི་མེ་ལོང, rmi lam brtag thabs 'phrul gyi me long) by Karma chags med (ཀརྨ་ཆགས་མེད, karma chags med), 2 ff. (MW23766_5DC139); *A method of examining the various dream signs* (རྨི་ལྟས་སྣ་ཚོགས་བརྟག་ཐབས, rmi ltas sna tshogs brtag thabs; ACIP, MW1ALS06558E); *The extended method of reading dream signs* (རྨི་ལྟས་རྟགས་ཐབས་རྒྱས་པ, rmi ltas rtags thabs rgyas pa) in the *'Bri lugs bang mdzod skor lnga*, vol. 41, pp. 186–195 (MW3KG218); *A profound instruction on examining dreams* (རྨི་ལམ་བརྟག་པའི་གདམས་པ་ཟབ་མོ, rmi lam brtag pa'i gdams pa zab mo) by Dam tshig rdo rje (དམ་ཚིག་རྡོ་རྗེ, dam tshig rdo rje), Mongolian National Library manuscripts (MW1NLM4232, MW1NLM4231); *The eighteen dream signs of the latter times* (དུས་ཕྱི་མར་འབྱུང་བའི་རྨི་ལྟས་བཅོ་བརྒྱད, dus phyi mar 'byung ba'i rmi ltas bco brgyad) in the *Chos rnam kun btus*, vol. 3 pp. 203–205; dream divination through a deity, Tārā, the goddess Śramaṇā, the Great Compassionate One and Mtsho rgyal (the *Sgrub thabs kun btus* and the *Gnam chos*); *A method of examining dreams, composed by Sakya Paṇḍita* (རྨི་ལམ་བརྟག་ཐབས་དཔལ་ས་སྐྱ་པཎྜི་ཏས་མཛད་པ, rmi lam brtag thabs dpal sa skya paN+Di tas mdzad pa; Mongolian National Library, MW1NLM933_A02C49, the attribution the manuscript's own); *Methods of examining various omens, drawn from Klong rdol bla ma's collected works* (ལྟས་སྣ་ཚོགས་བརྟག་ཐབས, ltas sna tshogs brtag thabs; MW29669_5E6E42); a modern collection of folk texts, *divination books, geomancy and the examination of dreams* (མོ་དཔེ་དང་ས་དཔྱད་རྨི་ལམ་བརྟག་ཐབས, mo dpe dang sa dpyad rmi lam brtag thabs; MW21880); and a modern article on "Klong rdol bla ma's way of examining dream omens" in *Krung go'i bod kyi shes rig* 1993 no. 4, pp. 66–80 (MW20242_55798C). The ritual norm in the tantric manuals is the dawn dream (ཐོ་རངས་རྨི་ལམ, tho rangs rmi lam): the disciple sleeps with his head toward the mandala and reports the dream of dawn (the *Rgyud sde kun btus*, 121 hits for the phrase, mostly biographies).

**Japan.** The printed almanac's annotations (暦注) carry no dream rule; none of the sources of [kyureki.md](../kyureki.md) names one. Dreams meet the calendar once: the first dream of the year (初夢, hatsuyume), now the dream of the night of 2 January, earlier the dream of the night of 節分 before 立春 (the *Sankashū*, Kamakura) and then of New Year's night; the picture of the treasure ship (宝船) laid under the pillow is an Edo custom, its sellers a sight of the year's end (日本大百科全書, *夢占*; Japanese Wikipedia 初夢; web, 2026-11-09). It is a dated observance (SPEC §7.3 lists none of this kind yet), not a reading of the day. The Edo compendia of calendar lore (大雑書, ōzassho), grown from the 暦注 under the influence of Onmyōdō (Japanese Wikipedia 雑書, read in the browser 2026-11-09), are the genre where dream reading would sit beside the calendar: a *Secret book of dream omens, good and bad* (夢占吉凶秘伝書, いろは引夢うらなひ) is reprinted beside a 大雑書 in 『京都大学蔵潁原文庫選集』 vol. 9 (臨川書店, 2018; NDL R100000002-I029406484), but whether the 大雑書 themselves hold a dream section (夢合) is unconfirmed: the Hokkaidō transcription of 『万代大雑書古今大成』 (伊藤孝行, 2017–2019) and 森田登代子's 「大雑書研究序説」 (日文研) were not reachable. The Chinese almanac (通勝, Tung Shing) is reported to carry a *Duke of Zhou's dream book* (周公解夢) section (English Wikipedia *Tung Shing*; a 1986 edition's section 18 "dreams"), the nearest case of dreams inside a calendar book; the dream book itself (the Dunhuang 新集周公解夢書, Tang) sorts dreams by subject, not by date.

**What follows for the app.** The dream omens of WB ch. 30 are built. The Svapnohana's rule by watch of the night is a short Tengyur text, read whole in the etext, that could be read on the scan and carried as a reading tied to the hour, if the owner wants dreams in the app at all; 初夢 could join the 旧暦 page's festivals as a dated line. Nothing found gives a dream reading by the day.
