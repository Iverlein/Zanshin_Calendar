# What is «བུར་སྙིང་ཐང» in the decoction for the wind-unripe fever: a sugar or a heart-wood?

Scan read at 3×; Yigdzin-1 reads རྙིང.

## Answered 2026-11-14

A sugar, not a heart-wood. The Serthang print reads «བུར་སྙིང་ཐང» (img. 257, l. 15, at 5×: ས over ཉ and the vowel sign i, so the question's སྙིང is the printed spelling and Yigdzin-1's རྙིང is not); the line is «རླུང་གིས་མ་སྨིན་སླེ་ཏྲེས་བུར་སྙིང་ཐང་། ཡང་ན་རུས་བཅུད་གསུམ་གྱི་བསྡུས་ཐང་བཏང་», for the wind-unripe fever the decoction of sle tres and «bur snying», or else the decoction of the three bone-essences. The commentaries spell the same word «བུར་རྙིང»: the Drikung dka' mchan (འབྲི་གུང་རྒྱུད་བཞིའི་དཀའ་མཆན; etext I1KG24731, p. 332) «རླུང་གིས་མ་སྨིན་སླེ་ཏྲེས་བུར་རྙིང་ཐང», and the Blue Beryl (བཻ་ཌཱུརྱ་སྔོན་པོ; etext I1PD411, p. 794, print p. 766) «རླུང་གིས་ཚད་པ་མ་སྨིན་ན་སླེ་ཏྲེས་དང་བུར་རྙིང་གི་ཐང». Neither glosses the word, but the Blue Beryl uses «བུར་རྙིང» elsewhere for a sweet foodstuff: «ཤ་མར་བུར་རྙིང་ཆང་དུ་བསྡུས་པ་སྟེ་བཅུད་བཞི» (meat, butter, bur rnying and beer, gathered as the four nourishing things; ch. 2 on wind, etext p. 596 f.), a beer «made of bur rnying» (བུར་རྙིང་ལས་བྱས་པའི་ཆང), and in the wind remedies as an addition to a decoction or butter («སླེ་ཏྲེས་བུར་རྙིང་བཏབ», «གོ་སྙོད་བུར་རྙིང་རྣམས་བཏབ»); in ch. 20 a fruit is likened to «བུར་རྙིང་བསྒོངས་པ», a rolled lump of bur rnying. In every one of these places the word is a sweet, nourishing thing taken with meat, butter and beer, or added to a decoction or butter, or rolled in a lump: a sugar (བུར, bur, with རྙིང, rnying, «old»: old, crude sugar by the word's parts; no published gloss was found). Nothing in these lines treats it as a wood.

The print's སྙིང in this word is a variant spelling of the commentaries' རྙིང (the Blue Beryl and the Drikung both have རྙིང at this line), not a second medicine; the Blue Beryl's etext has no «བུར་སྙིང» at all (its one string match, «ག་བུར་སྙིང་ཞོ་ཤ», is camphor followed by another word). The unsettled part is only the precise kind of sugar (which grade of unrefined sugar «old» names); no published gloss was found.
