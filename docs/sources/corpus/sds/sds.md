Chapters 1 to 16: the chapter of the tantra (4); the groups of medicines (11); foods (18); healing the fevers and pestilences (རིམས་ནད་གསོ་བ, rims nad gso ba) (28); showing the signs of death (འཆི་ལྟས་བསྟན་པ, 'chi ltas bstan pa) (49); healing the diarrhoea (འཁྲུ་བ་གསོ་བ, 'khru ba gso ba) (54); the illness of blood and phlegm discharged (ཁྲག་ལུད་པའི་ནད, khrag lud pa'i nad) (66); and so on to chapter 16 (116) with the names of the illnesses as the Tibetan gives them. Chapters 17 to 31 and the further works bound in the volume follow on pp. 20-23 of the etext and are not quoted.

What the page settles for the numbering: the contents count the chapter on the signs of death as the fifth and the chapter on the fevers as the fourth, and give the signs of death the folios 49-54. The print's folio is the etext page minus 24, so the chapter stands on etext pp. 73-78, as the body of the etext has it (sds:p72-73, sds:p75-77).

A person of the wind (རླུང, rlung) nature is dry, rough, thin-haired, restless in mind and body, talkative, and in dreams (རྨི་ལམ, rmi lam) flies in the sky. A person of the bile (མཁྲིས་པ, mkhris pa) nature greys early, is pale, sweats, is short-tempered, sharp of mind, and sees blazing fire in dreams. A person of the phlegm (བད་ཀན, bad kan) nature is steady in mind, firm in body, has many children, oily hair, and dreams of water and white things. A person of two or three humours is known by the mixed signs, the stronger one deciding the nature.

(1) After the signs on the body the text turns to the kinds of sign (མཚན་མ, mtshan ma). Bad dreams (རྨི་ལམ, rmi lam): the body anointed with oil; going south; riding a pig, a buffalo, a beast of prey, a donkey or a she-camel; a woman with loose hair and black clothes, bound with a red garland, led south laughing; meeting a gshin dog (གཤིན་དོག) or a non-Buddhist ascetic and keeping their company; drinking honey or oil; smearing the body with a salve ('jibs, འཇིབས) and dancing; falling from a mountain or crag; being bound or seized; crows pecking; climbing certain trees and an ant-hill; finding iron, tree-wool or the dregs of pressed oil; taking a bride; holding a garland of red flowers and wearing red; being carried off by water; eating boiled meat. A healthy person who dreams such things falls ill; a sick one dies.
(2) Good dreams: gods, brahmins, a banner, a parasol, cattle, a lotus, a king, white flowers, white cloth, clear water, a blazing fire, a living friend and the like; riding a horse, climbing a mountain, a tree with milk or fruit, a mirror, fresh meat, a garland found, crossing a great water. A healthy person gains wealth by them; a sick one soon recovers.
(3) The messenger (ཕྲིན་པ, phrin pa) from the sick person is read in the same way (p. 76): of low family, a limb missing, a non-Buddhist ascetic, holding a noose or a staff, red, black or old clothes, weeping, oil on the head and body, cutting grass or wood, a woman or a eunuch coming, riding a donkey, she-camel or buffalo: unwelcome; the opposite is good.
(4) The physician's (སྨན་པ, sman pa) own setting out (pp. 76-77): to hear an elephant, thunder or a drum, to see a jewel, a garland, a parasol or a full vase is good; birds with male names on the left and with female names on the right; a dog or jackal going to the right is good; a go dha (གོདྷ, an iguana), a snake or a serpent-spirit snake is always bad. The chapter closes with «འཆི་ལྟས་ཀྱི་ལེའུ་སྟེ་དྲུག་ལྔ །པ་རྫོགས་སོ།», the etext reading "sixth" and "fifth" together; the contents list it as the fifth.
