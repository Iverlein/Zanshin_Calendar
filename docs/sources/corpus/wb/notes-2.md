The Chinese pebble round (རྒྱ་ནག་རྡེལ་སྐོར, rgya nag rdel skor) is reckoned for the household as a whole: it cannot be done for fewer than four; above that, however many, it is reckoned for as many people as the house holds. For each person the twenty-four of cutting and tearing (གཅོད་འདྲལ, gcod 'dral) are laid and gathered into four deciding pebbles (བཏང་གཅོད, btang gcod), and with them each person's colour pebble of the mewa of the year (བབ་སྨེ, bab sme), five each. These are set out first for the men, from the eldest down to the youngest; then the women, however many, arranged the same way. Of two of the same year, the one born earlier goes first; if that is not known, the bigger or the older first. When they are arranged, exchanging the places of the life force's and the body's rows is the oral instruction (མན་ངག, man ngag). Then take the eldest man's first pebble, the body; then the youngest woman's last (ཁྲམས, khrams), the mewa pebble, and lay it after the first; then the first one's second pebble, then the last one's fourth, and so on, in a zigzag until all are taken, stretched into one row: that is called the plain pebble round. Then fold it up, placing the last pebble under the first, and fold while the rows are not yet equal: that is called the fold (ཁུག་པ, khug pa). If one is left over, it is called the leftover (རྡེལ་རོ, rdel ro) and set apart at the outer side of the rows. Then fold the double row up, placing the last of the second row under the second of the first [column]; then [p. 491] under it the last of the first, and so on, folding while the upper and lower rows stay equal in four rows: that is called the refold (ཡང་ཁུག, yang khug). If two are left over from it, they are the leftover, set apart at the outer side of the rows. Then, apart from the three counted down from the first in the upper and lower rows and the last of the rows, whatever lies between is cast off in one length. Of the top row, the god's places (ལྷ་ས, lha sa), three, and its last; below it the people's place (མི་ས, mi sa); below that the house's (ཁྱིམ་ས, khyim sa) and wealth's place (ནོར་ས, nor sa); the lowest of all the rows the enemy's place (དགྲ་ས, dgra sa): their verdicts, with the leftovers, are as the root text says.

Some reckoners' oral advice counts also all who gather at that place abroad and all who gather in the house that year. Some are seen to reckon five only, the four pebbles fought against the current year's life force and the rest alone, with the mewa of the year, without the twenty-four's deciding pebbles. This text does not do so. Though it comes to the same, divide the members' count of pebbles by three («གསུམ་གྱིས», so on the print): one left, it is the fold's leftover, the leftover of the god's and people's place; two left, the refold's leftover, the house's place's leftover; three left, one of the fold and two of the refold. Here the *Good Vase* (བུམ་བཟང, bum bzang) and others have the fold's and refold's leftovers mixed up, and to say that with two or one left there is no refold is coarse: with two left it is the fold of their own way and there is no refold, but in that way one cannot come to the fold, since one left must be the refold; otherwise, what contradiction is there if three left is made the fold? The point why it does not come so must be told. So in our way the fold's leftover is one only and the refold's two; another cannot come, because the rows are made doubled and quadrupled.
