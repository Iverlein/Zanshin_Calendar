Chapter 32 opens: "Geomancy, outer and inner, with the small remedies, set out in outline" (ས་དཔྱད, sa dpyad). A verse of homage and aim (l. 1 on: the knowledge of the all-holder of the reckonings, hard to grasp; the verse's own author-line names Khyung nag Shakya dar rgyal, with the figure-of-speech notes in brackets), then the reason for the chapter: monasteries, temples, a king's palace, ordinary houses and the graveyard all need a sound reading of the ground. The verse counts the earlier tallies of ground-signs (the sums of 5,600-odd, 25 dropped), then begins the method: the examiner, at dawn with a pure intention, takes refuge, rouses the mind of awakening, examines the ground's signs, and first tests the ground by smoke: incense smoke from a clean vessel should rise first to the right, turn to the left and go straight to the sky (good); the same over flour and over hidden smoke; smoke to the east is good, south and west bad, north middling, the four corners middling; then the ground itself is examined, abstaining from beer and the like, lying face up under the open sky.

The lines from «གཞན་ཡང་བྱ་རྒྱུད» on, in full:

"Further, the ways of examining the earth lord Lto-'phye (ལྟོ་འཕྱེ, lto 'phye) in the tantra classes (the *Legs grub* of the Kriyā class, the *Rdor phreng*, the *Gsang ba spyi rgyud* and the rest, as the line names them) differ from one another, and though many, are shown in the book [of their own]. The practice, in brief, in the essence: following the eight great lines in general, at the largest as wide as the earth, and at the smallest as little as a bre-measure's foot, the lines are drawn because one wishes to dwell within them; draw a square as large as is fitting. In it, from east, south, west and north, [the plot] is divided into ten great parts [«ཆ་ཆེན»] each way, and within them nine small parts [«ཆ་ཕྲན»] are laid in each: in each direction ninety squares, equal to the days of three house-months; the squares round the rim, all together, equal the 360 house-days. In this the earth lord Lto-'phye has a hood (གདེངས་ཀ, gdengs ka), his upper body a man's: the left hand covers the left ear, the right holds a jewel and rests on the hip; the lower body is the form of Lto-'phye. In the length of the body, ten *mtho* (མཐོ, *mtho*); in the width, twice his own face [«རང་ཞལ་དོ»]. Of these, the hood, face, heart, navel and secret place are one *mtho* each, five; likewise the serpent's tail is held to be five *mtho*. On the body the eight nāgas as told below. From winter's first month, the house of the Girl, starting from the fire [corner], clockwise, he goes one small part a house-day, and the head is south in the three winter months, west in spring, north in summer, east in autumn; at the fifteenth day of the four middle [months] he is at the Brahmā line of the direction. The way of taking the earth's aim and the accomplishment: for example, on the middle autumn month, at the middle of the east, the head east and the tail's end west; from the east, to the three great parts: for the hood, face and heart, small parts, twenty-seven; leaving those aside, below, from the Brahmā line at the north back, small parts, half and fourteen, before the part let go; the earth's aim is east-west a half and nine, south-north a half and five: these are the special places to take accomplishment. Further, small differences are told in the chapter on results. The signs are set and round them the three whites, three sweets and nāga tormas..."

The 1996 etext has «རེ་གདབ» for «དགུ་རེ་གདབ» (nine small parts each): the scan and Yigdzin-1 read «དགུ», and nine small parts to a great part is what the numbers need (nine times ten is the ninety squares of a side; three great parts are the twenty-seven small parts of the example). «ལྷོ» and «འཆད», which RD66 took from the etext against Yigdzin-1's «སྙོ» and «འཚན», are confirmed on the scan. The passage is the plot's frame and the figure of the earth lord in it; ch. 34 p. 387 uses the same figure («རང་མཐོ་བཅུ་པའི་ཚད») for the rite of digging (wb.2:p387).

The seven syllables are then elephant (གླང་ཆེན, glang chen), horse (རྟ, rta) and water buffalo (མ་ཧེ, ma he; so in Hopkins, the Rangjung Yeshe, the *Tshig mdzod chen mo* and the Mahāvyutpatti, Skt. *mahiṣa*, in Steinert's dictionaries); WB pairs «བ་ལང་མ་ཧེ» and «རྟ་བོང་མ་ཧེ» elsewhere (etext, vol. 1 img. 213; vol. 2 img. 109). So digging at the tail kills the large beasts and the cattle, and strength declines.

## Ch. 32 pp. 282, 287 and 294 against other texts and the scan: the serpent's walk, «ཆ་དགུའི་ས», the door table's first place, the lintel's verb (RD114)

*Read: etext (BDRC full-text search and etext windows, 2026-11-01) for the other works; scan, 2026-11-01, for the door table (Gser thang reprint I3CN5029 img. 293, enlarged, lines 5–8 of the table).* Read by RD114. These lines answer questions 418, 462, 463, 464, 469, 482, 483 in part; the passages they belong to are [wb.2:p282](#wb-2-p282), [wb.2:p287](#wb-2-p287), [wb.2:p291-294](#wb-2-p291-294).

Padma dkar po's collected works (BDRC MW10736, vol. 13, etext pp. 211–213, BDRC's etext only, 2026-11-01), in the rite of the ground of a Kālacakra maṇḍala, gives the serpent Lto-'phye (ལྟོ་འཕྱེ, lto 'phye) the same frame as WB's p. 281: the plot is a square of ninety cells a side; the serpent lies in its middle, nine cells wide, ninety cells long, with a serpent's hood; and he walks by the house-days (ཁྱིམ་ཞག, khyim zhag), one cell a house-day: on the first house-day of the first autumn month his head reaches the first cell of the east edge's north-east end and his tail the last cell of the north-west edge; in the three autumn months his head is to the east, his face to the south; in the three winter months head south, face west; in spring head west, face north; in summer head north, face east; so in one house-year he turns wholly round by head and tail. The place of the first stroke is then fixed by this position: "from the east, the hood and neck, three groups of nine cells; discard half and thirteen from the part pressed by the front; east-west nine parts, south-north the front of half and five groups: digging first there brings merit." Digging in the cells of breadth and length where he lies, or at his back, harms, and the verse of harm follows: «གལ་ཏེ་ལྟོ་འཕྱེའི་རྒྱལ་པོ་གནས་པ་ཡི། །ཆ་དགུའི་ས་ཡི་ཆ་ལ་བརྐོས་ན་ནི། །ཕ་མ་བུ་དང་བུ་མོ་སྤུན་ཆུང་མ། །མནའ་མ་རང་གི་སྐྱེ་བོ་མཛའ་བཤེས་འཆི། །གལ་ཏེ་རྒྱབ་ཏུ་བརྐོས་ན་རང་འཆི་ཞིང༌། །ཡང་ན་རང་གནས་ནས་འཕོ་ནོར་བརླག་འགྱུར། །མཇུག་མར་གླང་ཆེན་རྟ་དང་མ་ཧེ་ར། །བ་ལང་འཆི་ཞིང་སྟོབས་ཀྱང་ཉམས་པར་འགྱུར། །ཞེས་བི་བྷཱུ་ཏིས་བཤད་དོ།» ("so said Vibhūti"). It glosses the verse's «ཆ་དགུའི་ས» as «ལྟོ་འཕྱེ་གནས་པའི་ཞེང་དཀྱུས་ཀྱི་རེ་མིག་གི་ཆ», the part of the cells of breadth and length where he lies, and writes «མཇུག་མར» for the tail, ending «མ་ཧེ་ར། །བ་ལང་འཆི» (water buffalo, cattle die).
The same verse, with «ལྟོ་འཕྱེ་གནས་པའི་དགུ་ཆའི་སར། །བརྐོས་ན་ཕ་མ་བུ་སྤུན་དང་། །ཆུང་མ་རང་གི་མཛའ་བཤེས་འཆི། །རྒྱབ་ཏུ་བརྐོས་ན་རང་ཉིད་འཆི། །ཡང་ན་གནས་འཕོ་ནོར་བརླག་འགྱུར། །མཇུག་མ་གླང་ཆེན་རྟ་མ་ཧེ། །བ་ལང་འཆི་ཞིང་སྟོབས་ཉམས་འགྱུར།», stands in the etexts of the *Sa skya lam 'bras* (Go rams pa), the *Rgyud sde kun btus* (Rdzong sar print), the Sa skya chos mdzod chen mo, the *Bod kyi rtsis rig kun 'dus chen mo*, the *Bzo rtsis zlos gar*, the Rig med chos rig gter mdzod, the White Beryl's two prints and a *Dkar rtsis dang nag rtsis kyi gzhung* (BDRC full-text search, 2026-11-01); the White Beryl's own etext in Gser thang's print reads «ལྟོ་འཕྱེ་གནས་པའི་དགུ་མའི་སར། བཟོས་ན» in one place (OCR). Not one of them tells which cells the "nine parts" are beyond Padma dkar po's gloss.

- **The serpent's walk (q418).** Padma dkar po's account (above) is the serpent of WB's p. 281 with its calendar: a cell a house-day (ཁྱིམ་ཞག, khyim zhag), ninety cells a side, three house-months to a side. So the place of the first stroke is given by the house-day (season refined to the day), and the day's mansion plays no part in it. The *Bod spyi bshad* (bsb:p435-436) says the body moves "with the planets and mansions" and picks the working day by an accomplishing mansion; no text read ties a mansion to a part of the body. Longchenpa (lcm:p181-182) quotes the *Bam brgyad kyi 'grel pa* for the season only (head east, face south in the three autumn months, and so on) and adds that a blow on the head kills the master, on the tail the pupil, so the digging begins from the belly. Sādhuputra (sdp:p238-241) has the same four seasons. See the claim `ch32-serpent-walk-by-house-day`.
- **«ཆ་དགུའི་ས» (q482).** Padma dkar po glosses the verse's «ཆ་དགུའི་ས་ཡི་ཆ» as «ལྟོ་འཕྱེ་གནས་པའི་ཞེང་དཀྱུས་ཀྱི་རེ་མིག་གི་ཆ», the part of the cells in breadth and length where he lies. His body is nine cells wide on the ninety-cell square; the nine parts are best the nine cells of his breadth, so the place of harm is the ground under his body, not one small ninth part of a great part. This reading is the commentator's, not WB's own words, and rests on the etext, not on a scan. Narrowed, not closed.
- **The tail's «མ་ཧེ» (q463).** The verse in Padma dkar po's etext runs «མཇུག་མར་གླང་ཆེན་རྟ་དང་མ་ཧེ་ར། །བ་ལང་འཆི་ཞིང་»: elephant, horse, water buffalo (མ་ཧེ, ma he), cattle die, with ha and the vowel e; the *Sa skya lam 'bras*, the *Rgyud sde kun btus* and the Sa skya chos mdzod chen mo have «མ་ཧེ་བ་ལང»; the verse is called Vibhūti's. This confirms RD91's reading from the scan. The verse in them reads, line for line, as in WB.
- **The lintel's verb (q483).** The BDRC full-text search for «ཡ་ཐེམ་སྦས» has three hits: the 1996 print's etext (I1KG12907), the Gser thang reprint's etext (I3CN5029) and a copy in the *Bzo rtsis zlos gar* (MW1PD95727), where the line reads «ཁྱི་སྤྱང་བྲད་ཤུལ་ས་བླངས་ལ། །སྒོ་ཡི་ཡ་ཐེམ་སྦས་པ་ཡིས།» (the first two etexts «བྲང»; the third's «བྲད» is a misreading); «ཡ་ཐེམ་སྤྲས» has no hit. The earth is hidden (སྦས, sbas) at the lintel, not smeared on it: on this evidence, not on the scan, where the stack is still a blot. The etexts are not independent of the prints they copy, and I could not tell which of them were typed and which are machine-read.
- **«བུས་པ» and «བྲང་ཤུལ» (q464).** Nothing new on «བུད་མེད་བུས་པ»: it stays as RD91 read it. «ཁྱི་སྤྱང་བྲང་ཤུལ» is the same four words in both White Beryl etexts (the *Bzo rtsis zlos gar* copy has «བྲད»); no other text found uses «བྲང་ཤུལ». Dog or wolf-like dog is still not settled.
- **The door table's first place (q469).** On the scan (img. 293, lines 5–8) the east line reads «ཤར་གྱི་དང་གཉིས་གསུམ་པ་དང་། བདུན་བརྒྱད་ངན་ལ་བཞི་ལྔ་དྲུག། བཟང་»; there is no «པོ» after the first «དང་». The south line, «དང་པོ་གསུམ་པ་བཞི་པ་དང་», is seven syllables, as is each line of the table; with «དང་པོ» the east line would be eight. And each of the other three sides names all eight places, while the east would leave the first unnamed. So «དང་» is the first place cut short for the metre, and place 1 of the east is bad as already ranked. The page prints no digits (ordinal words only), so q462's numerals are the ordinals.
