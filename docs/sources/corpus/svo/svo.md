Page 268, lines 4–7, image 268 of I1344 (image = printed page; lines 1–3 hold the colophon of the preceding work, Vāgīśvarakīrti's *Deceiving Death*, "chapter four", which the etext also prints once more after this passage's first verses, see the note).

Title: in Sanskrit *Svapnohana* (the etext's «སྭབྣཱ་ཨུ་ཧཱ་ན»); in Tibetan the examination of dreams (རྨི་ལམ་བརྟག་པ, rmi lam brtag pa). Homage to the Buddha.

"The examination of the many kinds of dream I shall tell. A dream in the first watch (ཐུན, thun) of the night gives its fruit within a year; in the second watch, within eight months; in the third watch, within three months; in the fourth watch, within half a month: it will be seen, without doubt. At the very time dawn breaks (སྐྱ་རེངས, skya rengs) the fruit comes within ten days (ཞག, zhag). After this I shall explain the images; listen, virtue and non-virtue." Then the first images: riding an ox, riding an elephant; a mansion, a mountain peak, a forest tree; sitting in a boat and holding a lute; eating food and ploughing: always goods are gained. Being struck with weapons, the body covered with small living creatures, the body's dirt washed away, following excrement, blood flowing from the body: in dreams, when it is not a road taken, these are auspicious. Then the next verse begins: a king, an elephant, an excellent horse, a traveller (འགྲོན་བུ, 'gron bu), oxen and cows, seen "at the end of sleep" (གཉིད་ཀྱི་མཐའ་མ, gnyid kyi mtha' ma), continued on page 269.

Notes. (1) The etext of this page carries the sequence from the title to "at the end of sleep" twice (the first copy followed by the preceding work's colophon); the page's four lines hold it once: the scan shows the title at the start of line 4 and "…ཀྱི་མཐའ་མར" at the end of line 7, and the single copy runs 842 syllable-signs for four lines, the same density as pages 269 and 270 (about 205 a line); the duplicate is dropped from the Tibetan above. (2) Read on the scan: the layout, the title in line 4, the ends of lines 3 and 7; the syllables of the verses are as the etext prints them, not read one by one on the 1990-pixel scan. (3) «འགྲོན་བུ» is "traveller, guest" in ordinary Tibetan; the earlier summary's "cowries" is  not supported, a doubt.
(4) RD218: Yigdzin-1 and MITRA read the title's Sanskrit as «སྭ་བྷཱ་ཨུ་ཧ་ན» (Yigdzin-1) and «སྭ་བྷཱ་ཨུཏྤ…» (MITRA); the etext's «སྭབྣཱ་ཨུ་ཧཱ་ན» is not confirmed, a doubt. For the traveller both machines read «འགྲོ་ན་བུ» (the etext «འགྲོན་བུ»): the same word, a tsheg in a different place, not settled on the scan; the sense "traveller" stands as the etext's. Both machines read the verses' «བགྲོད་མིན» as the etext; "in the second watch" and "in the fourth" «བར»/«པར» differ only in MITRA. The scan's 7th line was cropped: the three verse lines at its end are there, their letters too coarse to settle the two spellings.

**Bya mdo Klu byams rgyal (MW8LS18598).** The verse is quoted twice. The first place (about pp. 110–111, in chapter 6 on dreams in Tibetan medicine) introduces it as what the *Rgyal ba'i bstan 'gyur* ("the Conqueror's Translated Treatises", the Tengyur) also teaches on the time at which a dream appears («rgyal ba'i bstan 'gyur rin po che las»), quotes the opening of the verse to the dawn line, and glosses: "looking at the watches of the night as they differ, the time at which a dream's fruit arises is named; by much looking and examining it is shown that a dream arising at dawn, in the tiger's time (tho rengs stag gi dus), surely gives its fruit". The second place (char. 318035, page not recovered; the book's title stands as a running head in the text near it) quotes the verse from the third watch to the dawn line, glosses it in the same words («tho rengs stag gi dus su rmi lam byung na 'bras bu 'byin nges par bstan pa ste»), gives the two lines with the etext's variants (for the first line «bo rengs sum cha phyis 'byung bas byed yin», where the first place has «tho rengs gsum cha phyis 'byung gsal byed yin»: OCR or the author's wording, not settled) and ends «as it is said» (གསུངས་པ་ལྟར་རོ); then it turns to illnesses that dreams foretell and quotes the Upadeśa Tantra's chapter on leprosy («man ngag rgyud kyi mdze nad kyi le'u las»: «spyi la phyi nang gsang ba gsum gyis brtag; phyi la mngon tshul rmi lam brtag pa dang», "the general: examine in three, outer, inner and secret; the outer shows itself by the examination of dreams", and «rmi lam chu dang shing dang ne'u gsing dang sbal ljong nyo sbrul 'bu sbrang sdig sdom sogs», dreams of water, trees, the bird ne'u gsing, frogs, snakes, insects, scorpions and the like (the English of the animal names is our reading of the etext's words)). The two lines «mtshan stod rmi lam brjed nges 'bras bu med; tho rangs mthong dang gsal na 'bras bu 'byin» are in the BDRC etexts of the Four Tantras' Explanatory Tantra tradition (hits in the *Gso rig 'bum bzhi*, the *Gsung rab sgo mdzod rin po che'i glegs bam*, Chos dbyings stobs ldan rdo rje's *Gsung thor bu*, the commentary *Bshad rgyud kyi 'grel chen bdud rtsi'i chu rgyun*, the *'Bum nag gsal sgron* and the *Gso bya lus kyi rnam bshad*, the last naming «bshad rgyud las»), so the author's «rgyud rang las» is the Four Tantras; their chapter was not opened here.

The author's own reading, a modern reader's gloss of lower rank than the texts: the watches are not set to hours; what he adds is that the dawn is the tiger's time (stag gi dus), i.e. the tiger's double hour of the Tibetan twelve (about 3 to 5 in the morning in the common table; our reading of «stag gi dus», not stated as a span by him), and that the first part of the night's dreams are lost or void and the dawn's, seen and clear, bear fruit. Neither book, in the parts reached, names a lunar day, a weekday or a season for a dream. The only clock-like span named is Padma's «skar ma» counts on p. 16, which concerns the length of a dream, not the watches.
