The Explanatory Tantra's (བཤད་རྒྱུད, bshad rgyud) seventh chapter in the Drathang print, img. 41 (the strip before gt:i42): the close of the sixth chapter with its colophon, and the opening of the seventh on the signs of the body's destruction (ལུས་ཀྱི་འཇིག་ལྟས, lus kyi 'jig ltas). Four kinds of sign, distant, near, uncertain and certain; the distant ones are the messenger (ཕོ་ཉ, pho nya), the dream (རྨི་ལམ, rmi lam) and the change of bearing (རྣམ་འགྱུར, rnam 'gyur). Then the verses on the messenger. A messenger who is a ya mtshan can (ཡ་མཚན་ཅན; the commentaries disagree on who is meant, see the claim), a monk (དགེ་སྦྱོང, dge sbyong) or of the physician's own kind (རིགས་མཐུན་པ, rigs mthun pa) is the sign that the sick will recover; the opposite does not succeed: one frightened, stiff, trembling, toying with stones and lumps of earth (བོང་བ, bong ba), calling from afar, doing evil, with unlucky ornaments and speech. Bad also is the physician's own state when the messenger comes: a rough mind, unlucky speech, cutting, tearing down, the offering for the dead (མཚུན་གཏོར, mtshun gtor), the burnt offering (སྦྱིན་སྲེག, sbyin sreg); and bad the day of setting out: the sixth, fourth and ninth of the lunar day, an eclipse of sun or moon, a bad planet or star, the night. On the road, bad: a broken or burning thing, the cry «kill», a cat, a monkey, a mongoose or a snake cutting across the road; good: vessels full of curds or grain, a bell, a lamp, flowers, the image of a god, white ornaments, one who keeps the dharma, a raised banner, a blazing fire, a horse, a sheep, a cow and the like with their young, pleasing sounds, food and drink, seen on the road or on entering the house. These are the signs of recovery. The strip ends at the road signs; the dream verses follow on gt:i42.

Commentaries on ch. 7's verses on the messenger (gt:i41), as BDRC etexts read on 2026-11-10 (etext only, no scan; lines joined, the etexts' own shads, spaces and slips kept, square brackets in the Blue Beryl are the editors' corrections).

The Moonbeams (ཟླ་ཟེར, zla zer) is the Tengyur's commentary on the Eight Limbs (ཡན་ལག་བརྒྱད་པ, yan lag brgyad pa), not on the Four Tantras. Its pp. 145–153 comment on the Eight Limbs' verses of the Body chapter (ལུས་གནས, lus gnas) on the messenger (ཕོ་ཉ, pho nya): the verses the Four Tantras' ch. 7 takes over in changed order and wording. The Moonbeams does not quote the Four Tantras' verses; the Four Tantras' line «ཡ་མཚན་ཅན་དང་དགེ་སྦྱོང་དང་། རིགས་མཐུན་པ་ནི་ནད་པ་སོས་པའི་རྟགས» is the Eight Limbs' «ཡ་མཚན་ཅན་དང་དགེ་སྦྱོང་དང་། རིགས་མཐུན་པས་ནི་གྲུབ་པར་འགྱུར» turned.

The Moonbeams' reading of the good messenger (p. 145). Ya mtshan can (ཡ་མཚན་ཅན) is an outsider, one who is not a follower of the Buddha (ཕྱི་རོལ་པ, phyi rol pa), abiding in a particular kind of vow-observance (བརྟུལ་ཞུགས, brtul zhugs); the monk (དགེ་སྦྱོང, dge sbyong) is one gone forth who keeps celibacy; the one of a like kind (རིགས་མཐུན་པ, rigs mthun pa) is one of a similar kind. If such a messenger comes, the cure succeeds. The messengers opposite to these are signs that the cure fails. So the Moonbeams takes the first of the three to be an outsider ascetic; it says nothing of a Brahmin and nothing of a monk of the Buddha's following for it. It glosses the verse that follows (frightened, stiff, trembling, rough, with weapon or staff, shaven, eunuch, long-haired, unlucky-named, evil-doing, a woman in a dirty garment, many at once, sick, ugly, with red garland and ointment, sesame-oil and mud-smeared, in worn clothes of bad colour, damp, in a single garment, riding a donkey, camel or buffalo, toying with wood and clods, calling from afar): «the physician does not go», «sets it aside», pp. 145–146.

The physician's rule (pp. 146–147, 152). The Eight Limbs states it three ways, each with the Moonbeams' gloss. (1) A messenger of the bad kinds above: «སྨན་པ་འགྲོ་བར་མི་བྱ», the physician does not go. (2) The physician at the time of the messenger's coming engaged in unlucky thinking or speech, sitting naked, cutting veins or wood, tearing down, the offering for the dead (མཚུན་གཏོར, mtshun gtor), the burnt offering (སྦྱིན་སྲེག, sbyin sreg), lying on the ground, loosening the hair, oiling himself, weeping, unrestrained: the sign that the sick will die (p. 147). (3) The sick and the messenger or physician alike in place and time (the same illness of the messenger's own; the physician sitting in the same kind of place at the same hour, a great messenger being seen to come to call him): «ནད་པ་དེ་ནི་གསོ་མི་བྱ», do not treat that sick one (p. 147; the cases by humour at the same page). The closing verse (p. 152): if a messenger and the like show unlucky signs, give that sick one up; otherwise, with a pure mind of compassion, strive to cure. The Moonbeams' gloss of «སྤང་བར་བྱ» is «སྤང་བར་བྱ་ཞིང་གསོ་བར་མི་བྱའོ», give up and do not treat. All these omens are the messenger's and the physician's own state and the road signs; none is the physician's own dream. The dreams it comments on (pp. 154–161) are the sick man's and say that he who dreams them does not live (མི་འཚོ, mi 'tsho); the Moonbeams gives no rule that the physician declines after a bad dream, and none that he dreams before the messenger comes.

The commentaries on the Four Tantras that quote it. Byang pa (etext p. 205, printed p. 169) after his own gloss adds «at length, in the Moonbeams on the Body chapter's sixth»: the same verse and gloss, almost word for word as the Moonbeams. Rin spungs pa (p. 229) cites the Moonbeams on the sixth chapter of the Body for the lines of the bad messenger («དམན་དང་འཇིགས་རིང་»); his own gloss of the good messenger (p. 227) reads the three differently from the Moonbeams: ya mtshan can is a monk, a gone-forth, a Brahmin of pure conduct, who has fully perfected the qualities of abandonment and realisation and works for the teaching and beings; the one of like kind is a man who has trained in the physician's line or holds some other branch of learning; he does not name an outsider. The Blue Beryl (pp. 198–199, printed pp. 170–171) says that ya mtshan can is the name of an outsider's vow-observance but does not occur in this region, and lists instead those of a like kind: yogis of the Drukpa school (འབྲུག་ལུགས, 'brug lugs) with their bone ornaments (the rendering of this clause is unsettled), monks who follow the Shakya king of sages, and the communities the Tibetan king Khri srong lde btsan's regular offering supported (the end of the clause is unsettled), then householders of fault-free form and clean habits and a fine horse; on p. 201 it explains with a tantra it names, «the tantra of the not-abiding in suchness» (དེ་ཁོ་ན་ཉིད་རབ་ཏུ་མི་གནས་པའི་རྒྱུད, de kho na nyid rab tu mi gnas pa'i rgyud) why ya mtshan can means outsiders (a list of the sects of the non-Buddhist ascetics), and p. 199 and p. 202 quote the Moonbeams by name for the gloss above and for the physician's state. Khro ru (VEIE0OPI4CEE4109_I5618, pp. 296–297) reads ya mtshan can as one of exceptional qualities, one gone forth with a virtuous mind; he does not cite the Moonbeams there. - Sitting naked; the hair and beard shaved off.
- Drinking beer (ཆང, chang) with the dead (གཤིན, gshin) and being dragged along by them.
- Adorned with red clothes and a red garland; dancing together with the dead (གཤིན་པོ, gshin po).
- These are the signs that the sick has gone into the power of the lord of death.
- These and the like are bad dreams; the passages of the mind's entry (ཡིད་འཇུག་བུ་ག, yid 'jug bu ga) are blocked by the illness, and so they are seen.
- The sick who dream them constantly die; for the one without illness it is not certain, and the rite of service (རིམ་གྲོ, rim gro) frees him.
- Six kinds: what was seen, heard and felt; a dream after a prayer; one that came true; and one that arose from illness.
- A dream of the early night is forgotten and has no fruit; one seen at dawn and clear gives its fruit.
- The good dreams, signs of long life and wealth: a god, the bull of the herd (ཁྱུ་མཆོག, khyu mchog), a man of renown; a blazing fire, a lake, the body smeared with blood or with filth; white clothes worn, a pennant, a parasol, a tree, grain found; climbing a mountain, a fine house or a tree with fruit; riding a lion, an elephant, a horse or a cow; crossing a great river or lake, going north-east; escaping distress, defeating an enemy; offerings made to oneself by the gods and by father and mother. Whoever dreams any of these has long life, no illness, and finds wealth.
