The questions sent to the regent, the hundred and thirty-seventh to the hundred and forty-second (*Removing the Tarnish*, the Taikhang reproduction, BDRC I5875 img. 100, l. 2, to img. 101, l. 5; read on the scan line by line in thirds of a line at 1–2×, against Yigdzin-1 and MITRA, which agree on 95–97% of the syllables; «[?]» marks a syllable the scan leaves in doubt). Each closes "offered as a request" (སྙན་སྒྲོན, snyan sgron).

- **137.** On the Chinese and Tibetan starting points (སྐོར་འགོ, skor 'go) of ch. 26: a doubt whether the five-pebble Chinese and Tibetan starting points taught by 'Chi med dga' ba (འཆི་མེད་དགའ་བ) and others, and the thirteen circles of examination (དཔྱད་ལྡིང, dpyad lding) of ch. 27 of this work, are two or one. Most old almanac-writers seem to know their names, but they cannot be practised; the commentary is to hand, but the root pebbles and the short commentary lie scattered, and before this nothing so ordered was available, so no practice was firmly settled. Then: the tenth, the reckoning of subjects and retinue (འབངས་འཁོར, 'bangs 'khor): is it reckoned with each subject's own year, with one chief person among them, or with the lord? This is in doubt.
- **138.** The eleventh starting point, the great horse reckoning («འདོ་ཆེན་རྟའི་རྩིས») and the rest, is reckoned with the owner's year-horse (ལོ་རྟ, lo rta); but with the chief owner, or with someone like the groom (ཨ་དྲུང, a drung)? And the short commentary's "all the pebbles speckled is good" (ཁྲ་ན་བཟང, khra na bzang) seems not to agree with the *thang shing*, the obstacle reckoning (ཀེག་རྩིས, keg rtsis), the reckoning of the dead and the rest.
- **139.** Ch. 27's circles are very easy to grasp and their results remarkable; when a question comes up, as of one gone abroad, there is no need to cast a small divination; the *spang rgyan lo ma* and Sprug's obstacle reckoning, which the Tsang reckoners praise, are there too. Yet some other ways count the circles of Balmo (བལ་མོ་ལྡིང་སྐོར, bal mo lding skor) male and female in opposite directions, like the progressed year (ལོག་མེན, log men): a doubt.
- **140.** The starting point of servants and workers is said to be counted from "the water bird", that is, the mother's bird; here "the fine starting point" is given: unless there is a proof that it is for servants, it is in doubt.
- **141.** Small reckonings such as the sixty *khog pa* («ཁོག་པ་དྲུག་ཅུ»), for example, from the heart's secret reckoning, the golden four legs spread out, an obstacle reckoning, and the birth reckoning's verse "when a girl is born on a *khon thog* («ཁོན་ཐོག»), the mother's door is blocked by the girl", are not put within their own reckonings: if they are to be given up from the start they are not needed here either; if not, are they ornaments added to the root *Gsal sgron* (གསལ་སྒྲོན) from other good explanations?
- **142.** Sprugtön's (སྤུག་སྟོན, spug ston) way of the obstacle reckoning given here: is it given because it has its root in this tradition, or as one way among others? For there are many more obstacle reckonings, the pebble-day thirteen and the rest.

The doubt restated: the foremost scholars of old held the circles of examination, or circles of Balmo, of ch. 27 to be one in meaning with the Chinese and Tibetan starting points, differing only in their names and in the five-start and eight-start ways (ལྔ་མ, lnga ma; བརྒྱད་མ, brgyad ma); and in the way of the upper Phugpa (ཕུག་པ, phug pa), of 'Chi med dga' ba and others, the earlier starting points, by the five elements of life force, body, power and luck, were held as an oral instruction; of these the fifteen Chinese ones, from "the man drawing the bow" for the harmony of mother and son, the marriage reckoning itself, and "the peacock with crossed necks" for the bride's going, the road reckoning, through to the rites and remedies, need nothing counted apart from the names that identify each starting point; and since the order of the fifteen Tibetan ones mostly matches the thirteen circles, they are known as one in meaning. In practice too, because most count the years from the iron bird for "the crystal garuḍa" (ཤེལ་གྱི་བྱ་ཁྱུང, shel gyi bya khyung), the common reckoners make the two the same as the circles.

The answer: examined in detail, the Chinese and Tibetan starting points of eight and of five, and the thirteen circles, must each be known apart, in the order this treatise sets them out. In the precious Lord Lama's (རྗེ་བླ་མ, rje bla ma) replies on the reckoning of stars and the Chinese reckoning («རྩིས་སྐར་ནག་གི་དྲི་ལན») it is said: above, the starting points of the thirteen Chinese circles of examination, the five-start way in general, as it was commonly known then; below, by the oral instruction, the thirty starting points of Chinese and Tibetan together, "turned from upon the iron bird and monkey". That means: if the eight-start and five-start ways, little known among people then, are turned by the oral instruction, they are turned from upon the iron bird and monkey, distinguished in detail by the male or female year of the one reckoned; and that shows the source for reckoning as this treatise now sets it out. It is not held that the circles and the Chinese and Tibetan starting points are one.

Then the second part: the difficulties of the starting points, hidden before, are cleared here. Whether the tenth, the reckoning of subjects and retinue, is reckoned with each subject's own year, with a chief subject among them, or with the lord, became a doubt because before there was neither an examination saying "it is this" nor a teaching saying "the practice is this"; so the answer is easily given. In general this falls under the power of the lord (དཔོན, dpon) himself, so it must be joined to the chief's year. The reason: the first start [check] is a reckoning of whether the retinue and subjects agree, the second of whether what the servants hold in their heart goes astray, the third of whether a thief of the mind toward the chief arises, the eighth of whether the servants sweep away the wealth; so the result of this reckoning is good or weak with respect to the lord. In particular: when not the chief lord but one belonging to that lord's household [asks] about subjects and retinue for his own wish, it must be joined to the year of that asker himself; the reason is that, having become a master, he asks especially about the result he himself wishes. No doubt remains.

The eleventh starting point, the great horse reckoning and the rest, does not go beyond being joined to the owner's year-horse; yet owners are told apart as chief and lesser: the year of the chief horse owner himself, the year-horse of a lesser horse owner, the groom (ཨ་དྲུང, a drung), or may it serve also for the reckoning of, say, a buyer? The question goes to the point. Examined well, this is in the power of whoever asks. For it has to show, for that one, whether in the place of his forefathers the horse wealth looks toward him, whether in the place of the last part of life it increases, and, in the middle, the present, harm by demons and the like; since it is for each asker's own matter, the results are not to be mixed. Here the chief, the groom and the buyer are to be understood without partiality, each for his own matter.

Further, on the secret text's short commentary, as set out here, "all the pebbles speckled is good": its reason. All white and all black are bad, as in the obstacle reckoning and the like; and "speckled is good" does not mean four white and four black. Examined in detail: on a base of black pebbles, white pebbles three, two, one and so on give, in order, a result better than plain black; and on a base of white, if one, two, three black and so on come, foretell in that order "very good" and so on. The reason: in the *thang shing* a speckled result is bad, white and black unmixed («དཀར་ནག་བསྔོ་མེད», not understood), and in the obstacle reckoning a speckled one must be shown with a result like a son meeting; so doing it this way is right. No doubt remains.

On ch. 27, the circles: their starting points are clear, free of the bad smell of guessing down and up (སྦུབ་བཀེན, sbub bken); no labour of seeking other reckoners for one gone abroad is needed; the *spang rgyan lo ma* and Sprug's obstacle reckoning are useful to the learned, and knowing them brings out the qualities of the *Gsal sgron*; so stating the meaning briefly is good. As to the doubt about drawing upward here, because some new compositions on the basis of the circles of Balmo count male down and female up, as the progressed year does: briefly, why it is not right that some Tibetan scholars and others repeat («བཟློས[?]») a male-down, female-up start for all the many starting points. It is empty of any scripture as a proof that all starting points are done so. If one says it is established by reasoning and is the special quality of the oral instruction: for particular starting points singled out this is understood; but then why is your oral instruction not applied to the *thang shing* and the others, some of which still remain? So the ground of it is called a mixture by analogy with the Chinese and Tibetan starting points.

If so, is not drawing upward the same: what proves that its root is pure? It is certain as a special case, as in the starting points handed down from Darchen Thigle (དར་ཆེན་ཐིག་ལེ, dar chen thig le), whose own annotation sets for the first "draw upward by the years lived" («དུ་ལོན་གྱེན་འདྲེན»); and this way is found in all the old texts. Moreover, [by the other way] the first starting point, the lord king's, would have to serve for the reckoning of the queen as well; if that is accepted, what need would there be for the seventh, from the mouth of the red bird of the eclipse, "for the noble ladies", the reckoning of women with the queen at their head? So what is set out here has a pure source. No doubt remains.

The answer to the hundred and fortieth (I5875 img. 497, l. 5, to img. 498, l. 3; read on the scan line by line in thirds at 1.3–2× against Yigdzin-1 and MITRA, which agree on 95–98% of the syllables; «རྦད[?]་ཁམ[?]» not understood; MITRA's witness on img. 497, l. 6, runs into a repeated loop and was not used there).

The proof that servants' and workers' starting point, given as "water bird" in the root, is set out as here, "the fine starting point", a different one: it was hidden before in the root itself. In the body of that text "water bird" is written; and at the end, after all the starting points are complete: "tiger, horse and dog: counted from the mouth of the pale tiger of the east, the unchanging nail of the east: from the earth male tiger. Rat, dragon and monkey: counted from the mouth of the blue turquoise dragon, the unchanging nail of the south: the water male dragon. Pig, sheep and hare: counted from the mouth of the black turtle, the unchanging nail of the north: the wood female pig. Bird, ox and snake: counted from the mouth of the red bird, the unchanging nail of the west: counted from the mouth of the fire female bird"; and it was left in doubt, "examine which starting point these are". So this is the servants' hidden starting point. The reason one knows it: the root says, "the tenth, by the four nails of the elements between, five starting points for servants and workers", which makes it plain; so this is held dear. [If one says:] cleared so, it is understood without difficulty, so no labour is needed: though much pride of the strong may arise from this, the earlier [doubts] are many, and they are cleared by the treatises of the Tibetan scholar Milam Gelek (མི་ལམ་དགེ་ལེགས, mi lam dge legs) and others. The answer to the hundred and forty-first, on the small reckonings of ch. 27 (I5875 img. 498, l. 3, to img. 499, l. 1; read on the scan line by line in thirds against Yigdzin-1 and MITRA; «ཕྲན[?]» where Yigdzin-1 has «བྲན»).

The doubt restated as above (question 141). The answer: the golden four legs belongs to the obstacle reckoning's results by the trigram (སྤར་ཁ, spar kha), and "if a girl is born on a *khon thog*, the mother's door is blocked" to the birth reckoning as a branch of it. Why are they told apart here, rather than [with] the sixty *khog pa* («སྤང་གནས་ཀྱི་རྩིས་ལོག་མིན་པ», not understood)? The *Gsal sgron*, which gathers the heart of all the obstacle reckonings spoken of here, did not state them among its essentials, and mixing these in would scatter the essentials, so they were kept apart; and since they are not to be given up, each one's essentials are stated unmixed and their kinds distinguished, and for those who want more these supplements are set apart, as the name "the chapter of small reckonings" (རྩིས་ཕྲན, rtsis phran) also makes plain. So the doubt is removed. The other additions are not like this; our own way's essentials are to be known with their meaning and commentary. By this example the reason why many small reckonings, the reckoning of times and others, are set apart is established. No doubt remains.

The answer to the hundred and forty-second, on Sprugtön's obstacle reckoning in ch. 27 (I5875 img. 499, ll. 1–5; Yigdzin-1, ll. 1–3 checked on the scan by eye in thirds, ll. 4–5 machine only; MITRA was not run on this page).

The doubt: is Sprug's obstacle reckoning set out as one way because of its root in this tradition, or else as one way among others, while the many others, the pebble-day thirteen, fourteen and eight and the rest, are not included? The answer: the two kinds of pure obstacle reckoning, scattered and gathered, do not agree as to purity, and it is hard to say "this tradition's way is kept as it is"; it is set out only as one way. The other ways are not set out because those known as ways are many. How many could not be told: even of those handed down from one master many differ; some because masters lacking the proper manner spoke at random with no ground; some because with many students the changes were many and many corrupt readings spread; some because, out of a wish for fame of wisdom, they spoke from their own conceit and kept far from purifying the master's [words]: who could tell them all? What is spoken of here goes beyond those: it is famous as Sprug Khyung (སྤུག་ཁྱུང, spug khyung), and is understood as unlike the forty-seven and the rest; so it was stated so. No doubt remains.
