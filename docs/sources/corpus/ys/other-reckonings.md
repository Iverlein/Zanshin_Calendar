The answer to the hundred and fifty-seventh (vol. 2, I5876 img. 16, last line, to img. 17, l. 4; the image is 8160 px wide; Taikhang margin number 545 on img. 17).

The question, as the reply restates it: the trigram reckoning is told in the *General Tantra* (spyi rgyud) thus: the three spring and the three summer [months] begin their dates with li, the hour of dawn is li, then khon and the rest follow the male circuit, eight to the day and eight to the night; autumn and winter begin with kham and, as the female circuit, rise in the hours [backward]; so in what way is what is said here (WB, set down after the Phugpa practice) the greater?

The answer: what comes from the *General Tantra* and the like is a pure tantra; but of the ways of the black reckoning that unfolded like leaves, the practice that is as the fruit, the essence, goes no further than Duhar Nagpo's *Clear Lamp* (གསལ་སྒྲོན, gsal sgron), and all, one's own and others', must wish it. Therefore from the *Speckled Sheet* (ཤོག་ཁྲ, shog khra), its very essence: "the tiger, horse and dog begin their dates with li, and the first time of the date's day is li too"; and, besides, from the first translation of the *Speckled Elements*, the *Vaiḍūrya* volume: "for the three, tiger, horse and dog, the year's trigram is li, so the month too is li, the first date li, the hour of dawn li." Since that same [text] is especially great by way of its marrow or essence, there is no cause for doubt.

The question: how do the reckonings set out in this work fall within the pebble count given above? If they do, then "rejecting" (ཁ་འཕངས, kha 'phangs) the joining of two bad ones told in the life reckoning (ཚེ་རྩིས, tshe rtsis), the birth trigram's (མ་ཀླུངས, ma klungs) and the rest, needs most of the life reckoning's pebbles, and the count falls short; if they do not, no need for the rejection is found.

The answer: the pebble counts of all the reckonings set here are known and their sum is stated. There, the reckonings that do not join two bad ones of the life reckoning, the *ma klungs* and the rest, are rejected; and that rejection need not agree here with the summed count of the life reckoning's pebbles: they can be gathered into the one pebble that identifies the rejection, and since that one falls within the total above, the pebbles are complete and the fault of a shortfall does not strike. To understand everything rejected one must know the life reckoning's pebbles; and if one rejected for each bad thing told there, that fault would indeed arise. So the doubt that the count falls short, or that the rejection is needless, has no ground.

So, by the regent, a couple is barred when both spouses' life reckonings are "utterly bad", and each life reckoning is judged as a whole: its places gathered into one verdict pebble whose colour is decided by its white and black pebbles; the single bad findings of the life reckoning are not rejected one by one.

The answer to the hundred and thirty-third (I5875 img. 491, ll. 1–5), read on the scan line by line in quarters at native size against Yigdzin-1; the scan's «ཁམས་རྡེལ་ལྔ» (l. 2), «སྤར་ཁ» and «ན་བ་ལ» (l. 4) stand where Yigdzin-1 reads «ཁམས་རྡེས་ལྔ», «སྦར་ཁ», «ནད་པ་ལ».

The question: WB's verse on identifying the illness (vol. 1, p. 435) reads "the four elements of the life force of year, month, day and hour; the elements of the four, year, month, day and hour, counted with li on tiger, horse and dog; and the element pebble of whichever is most: five". An inner reckoning has instead "the trigram of year, month, day and hour, and for the illness of the four times reckon the element of the five constitutions; wood is wind, fire blood, earth bile": that reads as the trigrams' elements of year, month, day and hour with the element of the four times' illness added, five. Do the two conflict?

The answer: "the element of the five constitutions for the illness of the four times" raised the doubt because medicine speaks of the times of phlegm and wind and the like; but it is not so. The plank-tree of the first translation of the *Speckled Elements* (ཁམས་ཁྲ, khams khra) has "first, the trigram of year, month, day and hour, and in the four times reckon the element of the five", and below it "the element pebble of whichever is most among them: five". So: on whichever year, month, day and hour one fell ill, the trigram elements of those four, and the one element most among them added as an element pebble, make the five. No doubt remains.

So the four pebbles of the identification are the elements of the trigrams of the year, month, day and hour *in which one fell ill* («གང་གི་ཐོག་ཏུ་ན་བ»), counted with li on tiger, horse and dog years, and the fifth is the element most common among the four.

WB's lines (vol. 2, p. 89, the tenth examination's strikes; the reply cites «ཐོབ» where the 1996 etext has «ཐོག»): "the strike of the three planets: born rat, dragon or monkey, li or kham reached; if one dies, the strike of the three Tuesdays gathered". The question: a man born rat, dragon or monkey who dies on li or kham is not struck by the Tuesdays. The answer: a man born rat, dragon or monkey **whose trigram has fallen (བབས, babs) on li or kham** («སྤར་ཁ་ལི་ཁམ་ལ་བབས་པའི་ཕོ་ཞིག») who dies is not struck by this fault, and the text does not say he is. Then why is it said here? It is told in the *Speckled Turtle* (རུས་སྦལ་ཁྲ་མོ, rus sbal khra mo); there too it is only an example, given for a woman; that does not mean a man is not reckoned; the reckoning of the four planets is chiefly reckoned for the mother, so here too it is told by a woman. So when a woman's [trigram] has fallen on li or kham [?], the small element (འབྱུང་ཆུང, 'byung chung) does not go beyond rat, dragon and monkey, and so the three Tuesdays gathered come. Some proud Tibetan scholars take the three as the rat, dragon or monkey day, Tuesday and noon: their own guess.

Then the trigram repetition's "when it has fallen on the *do gcig*, and the trigram of the *ma klungs*, and year, month, day and hour, four, are stacked": it means no more than this: the *do gcig* fallen on dwa (དྭ, dwa), the *ma klungs* born on dwa, and the year, month, day and hour of death on bird or monkey: the four of year, month, day and hour of death stacked in agreement with the trigram's element and life force.

So the regent reads WB's "dying on li or kham" in this examination as dying with one's trigram fallen to li or kham: the descended trigram (བབ་སྤར, bab spar), not the birth trigram; and in the repetition "fallen on the *do gcig*" as the descended trigram, beside the birth trigram (*ma klungs*), with the four times of death of the same element. Mipham's note on the same line reads it alike: the five pebbles of the four stacked on the *ma klungs* are the dead's birth trigram and the descended trigram of the year of death being one, and the life force of the year, month, day and hour having the birth trigram's element (wb.2:p504-512, read on the scan).

The answer to the hundred and sixtieth (vol. 2, I5876 img. 18, ll. 4–6; the image is 8160 px wide), read on the scan line by line in quarters at native size against Yigdzin-1, which reads «འཕུད་རྟ» for the scan's first «འཕུང་རྟ» (l. 5) and «འཚ་ལ་ལོ» at the end; «འདིར་ནོར་སོ» (l. 5) could also be read «འདི་ནི་ནོར་སོ».

The question: in refuting other ways of the obstacle reckoning (ch. 24) WB says the sky and earth doors are told in the thirteen gates of reckoning; but the thirteen gates set out under the reckoning of the moment (ch. 30, vol. 2, pp. 170–172) are thirteen up to «འཕུང་རྟ་རྔ་ཞོན», the ruin-horse riding the drum, and the doors are not among them; they come among the reckonings told after it: how is that not a fault?

The answer: there is no error. Up to the ruin-horse riding the drum are the thirteen gates proper; what follows them are the small reckonings (རྩིས་ཕྲན, rtsis phran) of the thirteen gates. So the chapter is known as the thirteen gates, and at its end the tantra itself says "the chapter showing the thirteen gates of reckoning, the fourteenth"; nobody can deny it.

So the regent, too, names the thirteenth gate the ruin-*horse* riding the drum («འཕུང་རྟ་རྔ་ཞོན», read twice on the scan), as WB's heading does; and the reckonings that follow it in WB, the ogre's and the demon's and the fixed sixty-year signs through the sky and earth doors (pp. 171–172), are the small reckonings of the thirteen gates, from the same chapter of the *General Tantra of Brahmā*.
