## <a id="zg-p285-286"></a>zg:p285-286 The household round in the Lotus Pond: one, two or three left by the folds

*Read: scan, 2026-10-25.*

> རྒྱ་ནག་རྡེལ་བསྐོར་གཟའ་མི་བྱིངས་ལ་བརྟག །མི་གྲངས་བཞི་ལས་ཉུང་ན་བྱར་མི་གཏུབ། །དེ་ཡན་དུ་ཡོད་སོ་སོའི་སྲོག་ལུས་སོགས། །གཅོད་འདྲལ་ཉེར་བཞི་ཁ་དམར་བཞིར་བསྡུས་སྟེང་། །རང་རང་བབ་སྨེའི་ཁམས་ཀྱི་མདོག་མཚུངས་པའི། །རྡེལ་གཅིག་དང་བཅས་ལྔ་ཚར་ཐུར་རིམ་སྒྲིག །དེ་ཡང་ཕོ་གྲལ་མོ་གྲལ་རྒན་གཞོན་རིམ། །གོང་འོག་མ་འཆོལ་རེའུ་མིག་བཞིན་དུ་བཀོད། །འདི་ལ་ལུས་རྡེལ་དང་ནི་སྲོག་རྡེལ་གཉིས། །འདུག་ས་བརྗེ་བ་མན་ངག་ལུགས་སུ་གསུངས། །དེ་རྗེས་ཨང་ཡིག་བཞིན་དུ་གོང་འོག་ནས། །[p. 286, img. 330] རིམ་བླང་ཐད་ཀར་བརྐྱང་པ་རྐྱང་པ་ཡི། །རྡེལ་སྐོར་ཞེས་བྱ་དེ་ཉིད་ཉིས་ཁུགས་སུ། །བཀུག་ལ་ཁུག་པ་དང་ནི་དེ་ཉིད་སླར། །བཞི་རྐྱང་བཀུག་ལ་ཡང་ཁུག་ཞེས་བྱས་ངོས། །གཅིག་ལྷག་ཁུག་དང་གཉིས་ལྷག་ཡང་ཁུག་དང་། །དེ་བཞིན་གསུམ་ལྷག་གཅིག་ནི་ཁུག་པ་དང་། །གཉིས་ནི་ཡང་ཁུག་རྡེལ་རོར་ཟུར་དུ་བཀོད། །འདི་ལ་ཁུག་པའི་རྡེལ་སྐོར་ཞེས་བྱའོ། །དེ་ལས་རྡེལ་ཕྲེང་དང་པོ་ལྷ་ས་དང་། །གཉིས་པ་མི་ས་གསུམ་པ་ཁྱིམ་གྱི་ས། །བཞི་པ་དགྲ་སར་ཞེས་བྱས་དེ་ཉིད་ལས། །དང་པོ་གསུམ་དང་ཐ་མའི་ཕྲེང་ལ་བརྟག །བར་ན་དུ་ཡོད་འདོར་སོགས་རེའུ་མིག་བཞིན། །འདི་ལ་གཅོད་འདྲལ་མི་བྱེད་གནམ་ལོ་དང་། །འཐབ་པ་གཅིག་པུའི་ལུགས་ཀྱང་གཞན་ལས་བཤད། །དེ་སོགས་རྣམ་བཞག་མཐའ་ཡས་འདིར་མ་བརྗོད། །ཞིབ་པར་གཞུང་དང་བླ་མའི་མན་ངག་དང་། །རང་གི་རིག་པས་ལེགས་པར་དཔྱོད་ཅིག་གུ།

Zhechen Gyaltsab's (ཞེ་ཆེན་རྒྱལ་ཚབ, zhe chen rgyal tshab) household round, in his table manual, the *Lotus Pond* (ཀུ་མུ་དའི་རྫིང་བུ, ku mu da'i rdzing bu; the same volume as the *Garland*, beginning on p. 262, etext p. 306), pp. 285–286 (I4PD2444 img. 329–330, image = printed page + 44). Read by eye on the typeset page (1400 px) at full size against BDRC's etext (pp. 329–330), which agrees but for two words: the scan has «ཞེས་བྱས» twice where the etext reads «ཤེས་བྱས», and the first line's «གཟའ་མི» (for «བཟའ་མི», household members) is so on the scan. Zhechen Gyaltsab was Mipham's pupil, and the verse follows the notes (WB vol. 2, pp. 490–491) step by step.

The Chinese pebble round examines the household members as a whole; it cannot be done for fewer than four. However many there are above that, each one's life force, body and the rest: the twenty-four of cutting and tearing gathered into four verdicts, and with them one pebble of the colour of the element of the mewa of the year of age, five in a column, laid down in order; the men's row, then the women's, by age, upper and lower not mixed, laid as in the chart. Here the body's and the life force's pebbles change places, as the oral instruction says. Then, by the numbers, taken in turn from the upper and the lower and stretched straight: the plain round. That folded in two is the fold; that again folded into a fourfold row is the refold. "One left, the fold's; two left, the refold's; and so, three left, one is the fold's and two the refold's, laid apart as leftovers." This is the folded pebble round. Its first row is the god's place, the second the people's, the third the house's, the fourth the enemy's; in each the first three and the last are examined, whatever lies between is cast off and so on, as in the chart. The way that does without the cutting and tearing and fights the year only is told elsewhere; the endless further distinctions are not told here.

The chart under the verse (p. 286) is the notes' laying and fold of five members, number for number (the laying with each of its five columns headed by a member, the rows body, life force, power, luck and mewa; the fold with "how the fold's and refold's leftovers are laid" under it and "here no refold leftover" at its side).

So Mipham's pupil gives the leftovers by the folds themselves, one, two or three left, with no division at all; and he names no speckled pebble in the round, though the lines just before (p. 285) lay a speckled verdict where a year-of-age place's white and black are even («མཉམ་ན་ཁྲ་བོར་གདགས་ལ་འབྲས་བཤད་བྱ»).

From the meadow's ornament leaves' way of laying (སྤང་རྒྱན་ལོ་མ, spang rgyan lo ma): in the gold year, whichever sky year has risen, its five kinds of mewa-sequence element, life force, body, power, luck and mewa (སྲོག་ལུས་དབང་ཀླུང་སྨེ་བ, srog lus dbang klung sme ba), are fought by the five of the one reckoned, the twenty-five cutting and tearing pebbles; with the five verdicts (ཁ་དམར, kha dmar) thirty. Likewise the sky year's down-count, the seventh, the six neighbours and the fourth executioner (བདུན་ཟུར་དྲུག་འགྲམ་བཞི་གཤེད, bdun zur drug 'gram bzhi gshed), each fought by five: thirty and thirty, so that all together, root-cutting, comes to a hundred and "nyer tham". The turquoise year likewise: the reckoned person's own progressed sign where it has fallen, its seventh, six neighbours and fourth executioner, fought as above: a hundred and twenty. The conch year: the month that has risen outside, its seventh, six neighbours and fourth executioner, four groups fought as before: a hundred and "nyer tham". All together, three hundred and sixty. It is said to be fitted to days and hours too: the year's and month's seventh and the rest follow the sky year's own mewa sequence; the progressed sign and its seventh follow the mewa sequence of the reckoned person's progressed sign. If wanted, only the later (the progressed sign's) head is taken. This is examined by life and lifespan, body and illness, power and food-and-wealth (ཟ་ཐང, za thang), luck and merit, mewa and the making of demons; and the verdicts are gathered into five general totals (ཁ་དམར་སྤྱི་བསྡོམས་ལྔ, kha dmar spyi bsdoms lnga). Whichever of them is white is good, black bad, in detail: a black life pebble, ways to raise the life and lengthen it; black body, the order of turning back its illness and demons; black power, remedies that increase glory, fortune and wealth; black luck, raising merit and strength, turning back men's talk. By mewa: one white, two the black demon (བདུད), three blue-black (མཐིང) the dmu-sen, four green the klu, five yellow, six white the earth lord, with the gods, seven red the btsan, eight white the owner of the treasury, the king, nine the ma mo; their remedies by Dharma, mantra, Bon and gto rite are known from what was said above.

On «ཉེར་ཐམ» (RD70, 2026-10-28): the scan prints it so, in the gold year's sum (img. 344, the third line of the verse's last part: «ཡོངས་དྲིལ་རྩ་གཅོད་བརྒྱ་དང་ཉེར་ཐམ་འབྱུང») and in the conch year's (top of img. 345: «སྔར་བཞིན་འཐབ་པས་བརྒྱ་དང་ཉེར་ཐམ་བཅས»), and «བརྒྱ་དང་ཉི་ཤུའོ» for the turquoise year's (img. 344, last lines). Zhechen Gyaltsab's *Garland of White Utpalas*, the bases and the way of reckoning of the thirteen circles (pp. 303–304; BDRC's open scan I4PD2444 img. 347–348, image = printed page + 44, read by eye on the typeset page against BDRC's etext, VEIE0OPIDB78C0F0_I4PD2444, etext pp. 347–348, which it matches in the lines quoted; the text goes on to p. 313 with each circle's readings, white and black, and their remedies, and closes as written by Zhechen-pa Jamyang Lodrö Gyatso (ཟེ་ཆེན་པ་འཇམ་དབྱངས་བློ་གྲོས་རྒྱ་མཚོ, ze chen pa 'jam dbyangs blo gros rgya mtsho) as a branch of his table manual, the *Kumuda Pond* (ཀུ་མུ་དའི་རྫིང་བུ, ku mu da'i rdzing bu)). Found with BDRC's full-text search for «བལ་མོ་ལྡིང་སྐོར», the only worked circle the search gave.

It has three parts: the basis, the way of reckoning and the results. The basis, thirteen kinds: (1) the white crystal garuḍa: the earth lord, the king, counted from the bird of the element's enemy; (2) the white conch-lion of the snows: the lama and spiritual friend, from the element's enemy's hare; (3) the yellow golden snake of existence: the knowledge-holder, the mantra-holder, from the friend's snake; (4) the blue turquoise dragon of the sky: the Bon priest, from the mother's dragon; (5) the steadfast elephant of the earth: ministers and officials, from the friend's ox; (6) the striped Chinese tiger of the forest: young men in their prime, from the enemy's tiger; (7) the red bird: noble ladies and women, from the element's mother's rat; (8) the blazing wish-fulfilling jewel: small children and youths, from the enemy's tiger; (9) the blue young peacock: young girls, from the mother's dog; (10) workers and servants, two ways: roughly, the golden bird, from the mother's bird; finely, for tiger, horse and dog the friend's tiger, for rat, dragon and monkey the mother's dragon, for pig, sheep and hare the pig that agrees with the power's element (དབང་ཁམས, dbang khams), for bird, ox and snake the son's bird; and that, it is said, is applied to the master's year; (11) the long-horned yellow divine sheep: land, country and treasury, from the enemy's pig; (12) the golden vase: dry goods, gold, silver, copper, iron, turquoise and the like, from the friend's pig; (13) the great wild yak in its prime: horses, sheep, cattle and the blooded beasts, from the mother's ox.

Second, the way of reckoning. For example, if the earth lord is a wood monkey, from the iron bird, the enemy of his element: from whatever each one's basis is, count upward the years of the one reckoned, as many as he has lived (དུ་ལོན, du lon); here there is no difference of male, female or the like. The life force, body, power, luck and mewa of the year where it falls are fought by the life force, body, power, luck and body mewa (ལུས་སྨེ, lus sme) of the one reckoned. For example, a wood monkey and an iron bird: the life force meets itself, the body is the son, the power becomes the enemy. A meeting of mother or friend, and of earth or water with itself, lays a white pebble; of son or enemy, and of wood, iron or fire with itself, a black one.

So the *Garland* starts the first circle of a wood-monkey king from the iron bird, iron being wood's enemy, and the wood is the king's power (the wood of the wood monkey, the element the year is named by); it is his luck too (a monkey's luck is wood), so the example alone does not choose between the two, but the tenth circle's fine way names the power (དབང་ཁམས) outright, as the notes do (vol. 2, p. 533). It counts upward by the years lived for every person, man or woman; and it fights the reached year's mewa with the reckoned person's body mewa (claim circles-zg-worked).
