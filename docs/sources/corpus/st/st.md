## <a id="st-p21"></a>st:p21 Ch. 2 of the Explanatory Tantra, the child in the womb: the mother's dream of a man marks a boy

*Read: scan, 2026-11-09.*

> ཟླ་དགུ་འདས་ནས་བཙའ་བའི་དུས་ཡིན་ཏེ། དེ་ཡང་གཡས་ལོགས་ལ་བརྟེན་དཀུ་གཡས་མཐོ། ལུས་ཡང་རྨི་ལམ་དག་ན་སྐྱེས་པ་མཐོང་། ནུ་ཞོ་གཡས་པ་སྔར་འབྱུང་ཁྱེའུ་སྐྱེ། སྐྱེས་པ་ཕྲད་འདོད་གླུ་གར་རྒྱན་ལ་དགའ། གོང་ལས་ལྡོག་པ་བུ་མོ་སྐྱེ་བའི་རྟགས། དེ་དག་འདྲེས་ལས་མ་ནིང་འབྱུང་པ་སྟེ། དབུས་དམའ་མཐའ་གཉིས་མཐོ་ན་མཚེ་མར་སྐྱེ།

Serthang p. 21, img. 34, ll. 8–12 of the scan (the passage is the end of the chapter's account of the child in the womb, the second chapter by its colophon on p. 22 of the print, on the formation of the body, ལུས་ཀྱི་ཆགས་ཚུལ, lus kyi chags tshul, the Explanatory Tantra, བཤད་རྒྱུད, bshad rgyud, by the run of colophons that begins again at the second after the Root Tantra's sixth on etext p. 28). When the nine months are over it is the time of birth. The mother lies on her right side with the right hip high. The body is light (ལུས་ཡང, lus yang, a dictionary headword: "the body is light"), and if in her dreams (རྨི་ལམ, rmi lam) she sees a man (the word between, དག, dag, whether the plural marker or "clear, pure" is not settled), it is a boy; the right breast gives milk first and a boy is born; she wishes to meet men and delights in song, dance and ornaments. The reverse of these is the sign of a girl. A mixture of them gives a child who is neither (མ་ནིང, ma ning). If the middle is low and both sides are high, twins are born.

Read on the scan, 2026-11-09. The etext and Yigdzin-1 agree with the scan in every line. The print's page number is the image minus 13 here (the registry's rule for the first pages). RD227 (2026-11-09): the line (the eleventh row of the scan, 3548 px wide at 2×) read syllable by syllable on a 4× crop: ལུས་ཡང་རྨི་ལམ་དག་ན་སྐྱེས་པ་མཐོང་, every letter clear, ཡང with ཡ over ང and དག with ད over ག; the etext reads the same. The Drathang woodblock print (GT, img. 29, the fifth row, a crop at x 1090–1520, y 180–235, 4.5×) has the same words in the same order. So the letters are settled on two prints. ཡང is "light": the pair "lus yang" is a headword of Jim Valby's dictionary ("light body, the body is light") and ཡང་བ is "light, the opposite of heavy" in several; it sits among body-signs (side, hip, body, breast), so it is the body, not "also". དག stays open between the plural marker after "dreams" and "clear, pure" (the dictionaries give both); the dream of a man is a sign of a boy on either, but "clear" would restrict it to a clear dream. GT's interlinear red notes did not resolve at 1763 px.

What six commentaries say of the Explanatory Tantra's two lines «མཚན་སྟོད་རྨི་ལམ་བརྗེད་ངེས་འབྲས་བུ་མེད། ཐོ་རངས་མཐོང་དང་གསལ་ན་འབྲས་བུ་འབྱིན།» (ch. 7; the Serthang pages 31–32 are not on the scan, so the lines are not given on this page; claim st-dream-night-first-part-dawn), as BDRC etexts read on 2026-11-10 (etext only; padding marks dropped).

**Byang pa**, p. 219: the lemma has «བརྗེད་ངེས» and, in the etext, «ཐོ་རང་» for ཐོ་རངས. Generally the dream of the first part of the night (མཚན་སྟོད, mtshan stod) is unclear and what arose is forgotten, so no fruit; a dawn dream (ཐོ་རངས, tho rangs), a good one or a bad one, if clear and not forgotten, brings good or bad fruit. "In detail", he goes on, see the Moonbeams' gloss "just above": the lines that follow are the Moonbeams' (st:p31-32b).

**Rin spungs pa**, p. 239: the first part's dream is unclear, and heavy sleep presses after the dream, so the earlier dreams are forgotten («བརྗེད་ངས»), their fruit is not seen, no fruit; dawn is the time when the wind-mind of most beings enters the central channel (དབུ་མ, dbu ma), the constituents are clear and pure and sleep is thin, so a dream then, good or bad, if not forgotten, brings fruit as if seen before one. He then quotes the Moonbeams' lines (st:p31-32b). The etext's lemma «བརྗེད་ངན» (brjed ngan) differs from the «བརྗེད་ངས» of his own gloss.

**The Drikung dka' mchan**, p. 81: no lemma; the first part's dream forgotten (བརྗེད་ངས་པ) is a delusion (འཁྲུལ་པ, 'khrul pa), without fruit; a dream seen at dawn, if it is clear, surely brings fruit.

**Skyem pa Tshe dbang**, p. 190: «ནམ་ཕྱེད་མཚན་སྟོད», midnight and the first part of the night: the dream is with forgetfulness (བརྗེད་ངས), not remembered, no fruit; seen at dawn, clear and not forgotten, it brings fruit.

**Zur mkhar ba**, p. 356, and **the Blue Beryl**, p. 216, in the same sentences (the Blue Beryl's differ by «གང་དམིགས་ཀྱི» for his «གང་རྨིས་ཀྱི»): of the six kinds of dream, whichever is dreamt in the first part of the night, which is dusk (སྲོད་ལ, srod la), is not cut by other sleep and becomes forgotten, so neither good nor bad fruit comes; midnight (ནམ་ཕྱེད, nam phyed) is of a kind with it; at the close of dawn (ཐོ་རངས་ཀྱི་མཇུག་ཏུ) any dream seen, with sleep not breaking in again, and with nothing following that changes it, staying clear in the mind, brings the fruit of what was seen for certain.

None of the six names the stretch from midnight to dawn; the times they name are dusk, midnight, and dawn (its close).

The Eight Limbs' verse «མཚན་སྟོད་རྨི་ལམ་འབྲས་བུ་རིང། མཐོང་བ་ཆུང་ངུ་བྱེད་པ་ཡིན།» ("a dream of the first part of the night: its fruit is far, what is seen is made small") with its gloss in the Moonbeams and in the three commentaries on the Four Tantras that take it over (BDRC etexts, 2026-11-10, etext only).

The Moonbeams' gloss: «མཚན་སྟོད་ཕྱེད་པ་ཡན་ཆད་རྨི་ལམ་རྨིས་པ», a dream dreamt "up to the half" (ཕྱེད་པ, phyed pa) of the first part of the night has its fruit far off, and what is seen is for the most part small and weak. Then the next lines of the verse: the dream seen at daybreak, when the cattle are let out to graze (བ་ལང་གཟས་ལ་གཏོང་བའི་ཚེ), with no sleep after it and no unfitting talk between, brings great fruit that very day; and giving (སྦྱིན་པ) and burnt offering make a bad dream's fruit small.

Zur mkhar ba (p. 351) sets the same lemma lines and the same gloss, with one change: «མཚན་སྟོད་ཅེས་པ་ནམ་ཕྱེད་ཡན་ཆད་དུ», "what is called the first part of the night: dreaming up to midnight". The Blue Beryl (p. 209) has this sentence of his without the lemma lines; its «མཐོང་བཞིན་པ» for the Moonbeams' «མཐོང་བ་ཞན་པ» ("weak") is a slip of the etext or the print. Byang pa (p. 219) keeps the Moonbeams' wording «མཚན་སྟོད་ཕྱེད་པ་ཡན་ཆད» and quotes it directly after his gloss of the Four Tantras' lines.

So the Moonbeams' own words leave the span open (up to the half of the first part, or up to the half of the night), and Zur mkhar ba, followed by the Blue Beryl, fixes it as "up to midnight". This gloss is about the Eight Limbs' verse; in these commentaries the Four Tantras' lines stand in st:p31-32.

**The humours' verse** (Blue Beryl p. 211, Zur mkhar ba p. 354, in the same words): on what makes things appear as they do in the dream, "at dusk (སྲོད, srod) the phlegm is mostly strong; at midnight (ནམ་ཕྱེད, nam phyed) the bile is said [to rule]; dawn (ཐོ་རངས) is the very time of wind; then, by the nature of the constituents (ཁམས, khams), the dream appears as the object", «ཞེས་བྱ་བ་ལ་སོགས་པ་རྒྱུད་ནས་མང་དུ་གསུངས་པའི་ཕྱིར», "because this and the like is said much in the Tantra (རྒྱུད་ནས)"; the Blue Beryl copies Zur mkhar ba here, and neither names a tantra. The verse is not in Byang pa, Rin spungs pa, the Drikung, Skyem pa or the Moonbeams (their etexts searched for its lines). BDRC's search finds its lines in Rang byung rdo rje's Collected Works and Kong sprul's commentary on his Zab mo nang don, both introduced as "from the Tantras" («rgyud rnams las», «rgyud las»), and in the third volume of a Gso ba rig pa collection (search only). It is a rule of the dream's content by the part of the night and the humour, not of its fruit.

**The Root Tantra's rhythm** (Blue Beryl p. 75, on the leaves of the tree of illness, the times of the rise of each humour): wind illnesses mostly rise in the summer season and at the evening (དགོང, dgong) and dawn of a day; bile in autumn and at midday (ཉིན་དགུང) and midnight (མཚན་དགུང, mtshan dgung; the etext brackets «གུང»); phlegm in spring and at dusk (སྲོད) and early morning (སྔ་དྲོ). Midnight and dawn are both named there, as the times of bile and of wind; the stretch between them is not.

**Klong rdol bla ma Ngag dbang blo bzang's verse** (ACIP etext, p. 3): it begins by saying it sets out, "as taught in Ma gcig's *Zab mo gcod kyi dmigs 'bum*", the fruits of dreams, good and bad; examine well when one is not sick, in pain or disturbed in mind. "The night is divided into three thirds by the watches (ཐུན, thun): the dusk third is the habit-traces (བག་ཆགས, bag chags) of the earlier; the midnight third (གུང, gung) is the play of demons (བདུད་སྲིན, bdud srin); the dawn third is what clarifies what arises later (ཕྱིས་འབྱུང་གསལ་བྱེད, phyis 'byung gsal byed)". Then: what comes at dusk is mostly from earlier deeds, attachments, places, companions, food, talk, near ones, so its habit-trace arises within three days and it is dreamt again and again, some from earlier lives, none fixed; in the midnight third, demons, rākṣasas, asuras, māras roam, so whatever, good or bad, appears is their disturbing play, and nothing is fixed, the wise say; and «ཐོ་རངས་ཁ་ཡིན་ནམ་གྱི་གསུམ་ཆ་ལ། བཟང་ངན་འབྲས་བུ་འབྱུང་ཕྱིར་བརྟག་པ་གཅེས།», "at the mouth of dawn, the third of the night, since good and bad fruit arises, judging is precious". So «གསུམ་ཆ་ཕྱིས་འབྱུང་གསལ་བྱེད་ཡིན» says of the dawn third that it is the clarifier of what arises later: the one third of the three in which the dream shows what comes. The colophon: Klong rdol bla ma Ngag dbang blo bzang wrote it (the BDRC record names the same author).

**Bya mdo Klu byams rgyal** (search snippet only): after the dawn time he says that a dream arising at dawn is shown to bring its fruit surely, "as is said" with the Klong rdol line, and then «rgyud rang las», "the Tantra itself says" with the Four Tantras' first line, with «brjed nges». He sets the two as two authorities, and the first is not called the Tantra's.

**From midnight to dawn** as a span: «ནམ་ཕྱེད་ནས་ཐོ་རངས་ཀྱི་བར», "from midnight to dawn", is a stock phrase in tantric practice texts (the snippets above), where that span is the time of seizing the clear light of sleep; the search for it among the commentaries on the Four Tantras found no use of it for dreams.

Serthang pp. 185–186, img. 258 and 257, from the sentence «བཅོས་པའི་ཐབས་ལ་སྤྱི་དང་བྱེ་བྲག་གཉིས» on to the foot of p. 186 (the treatment of the unripe fever, ཚ་བ་མ་སྨིན་པ་བཅོས་པ, tsha ba ma smin pa bcos pa, the fifteenth chapter, whose dream line is on p. 183, st:p183; the printed pages run backwards, so the treatment lies on the lower images 258 and 257). The chapter has no remedy for the dream (རྨི་ལམ, rmi lam) itself; what it gives is the treatment of the whole unripe fever the dream belongs to. The way of treating (བཅོས་པའི་ཚུལ, bcos pa'i tshul): the fever of pestilence (རིམས, rims) is ripened like beer, the border-troubled fever (འགྲམས་འཁྲུགས, 'grams 'khrugs; sense not settled) is sorted out like seizing an enemy. The means (གཉེན་པོ, gnyen po) are four: medicine, external treatment, food, conduct. Medicine: the decoction of the four manu (མ་ནུ་བཞི, ma nu bzhi), warm and mild; where blood and bile are strong, a mild decoction of the three fruits (འབྲས་བུ་གསུམ, 'bras bu gsum) with sle tres (སླེ་ཏྲེས); for a long unripeness, powders (བིག་པན, the word read so by Yigdzin-1, ཐིག་པན in the etext; not settled) with ash and the three good ones (བཟང་པོ་གསུམ, bzang po gsum); steaming of the stomach, rubbing of the four soles; food: thin flour, gruel, boiled cold water, nothing rich, easy to digest, warm and mild; boiled water at evening and night; conduct: warm clothes, sitting between sun and shade. To be avoided: violent exertion, being scorched by fire, sleep by day, meat, beer, heating and rich food, the cold and damp ground, sour milk, cold water, hard food, and at this time purging, emetics, sweating and cooling decoctions, which send the fever off its course. Those who do not know the means of ripening treat it as easily ripe with warmth and raise the illness's strength; others treat the unripe fever with cooling, like killing a guest who came with a friend (the text's own saying); the antidote, laid on the helper, does not kill the bile-heat and wind. Helpers strengthen the fever itself, so the ripening means are balanced between cool and warm; then what has been born is cleared and none is born later. Page 186: for the fever that ripens of itself, the strong one gets the ripening antidotes in due measure; the weak one, the fever being like a bird, the decoction of the three fruits with tig ta (ཏིག་ཏ), li ga dur (ལི་ག་དུར), ba le ka (བ་ལེ་ཀ), utpala (ཨུཏྤལ) and sle tres, taken lukewarm, with sweating, the face covered and the sweat covered with wool against the wind, a steam from nasal vapour if it will not come; no sweat is a great danger. Sweating for three days, then three days of washing the body, then the three cooling powders; this turns the fever back from its path. For the fever with no time to ripen: in the wind's time ripening decoctions, in bile's time cooling antidotes; waiting for the ripening takes the life; so look to the illness and the medicine, not to the turbidity. > ནད་ཀྱི་གྲང་རླུང་མཁལ་མའི་དྲོད་ཉམས་པས། རྩ་ཁ་མ་ཐུབ་ཆུ་དང་བསྡོངས་ནས་འཛག བཅུ་གསུམ་བཅུ་དགུ་ནང་ལོང་གདོང་ངོས་བསྲེག བོང་ཤ་སྲམ་ཤ་ད་བྱིད་སྨན་མར་སྦྱར། ཚ་བ་ཚྭ་སྣའི་རིལ་བུ་ཆང་གིས་དབུལ། དུག་བྱུང་ཕྱི་ནང་གསང་རྟགས་ངོས་བཟུང་ལ། དེ་ཡི་གཉེན་པོས་བསད་ཅིང་ལུས་ཟུངས་བསྲུང་། གདོན་ཡིན་རྨི་ལམ་བུད་མེད་འདྲེན་པར་འགྱུར། མོ་ཕྱུ་རིམ་གྲོ་རྟེན་འབྲེལ་(བ་རའི་འོ་མར་དྲི་ཆེན་སྦྱར་བ་རྐང་མཐིལ་ལ་བྱུག) སྔགས་ཀྱིས་གསོ(བ་ནི་ལུག་ཐོང་གི་བལ་མི་ལག་མ་འགྲིམས་པ་ཁྱེའུ་གཙང་མས་བཀལ་བའི་སྲད་བུ་ལ་རྡོ་རྗེའི་མདུད་པ་བདུན་བྱས་ཏེ་སྔགས། ཨོཾ་ཧ་ར་ཧ་ར་བྷན་དྷ་ནི་ཤུ་ཏ་དྷ་རི ··· ནི་ཐ་ར་ཏེ་སྭཱ་ཧཱ། སྟོང་བཏབ་ལ་བཅིང།) དགོས་པ་ནད་མེད་ལང་ཚོར་ལྡན་པ་ཡིས། དུས་ཚིགས་རྣམས་སུ་ཉལ་པོ་དགག་པ་མིན། འཕྲལ་དུ་སེམས་དགའ་བུ་ཚའི་རྒྱུད་སྐྱེད་ཅིང། སྟོབས་ལྡན་བུད་མེད་ཀུན་ལ་ཐོགས་མེད་འགྲོ། འདོད་ཡོན་ཀུན་ལས་ཆགས་པ་མཆོག་ཏུ་གྲགས། ཞེས་གསུངས་སོ། བདུད་རྩི་སྙིང་པོ་ཡན་ལག་བརྒྱད་པ་གསང་བ་མན་ངག་གི་རྒྱུད་ལས་རོ་ཙ་བར་བྱ་བའི་ལེའུ་སྟེ་དགུ་བཅུ་རྩ་གཅིག་པའོ།

Serthang p. 493, img. 507, ll. 8–20 of the scan (the chapter on making the flavour, རོ་ཙ་བར་བྱ་བ, ro tsa bar bya ba, the ninety-first of the Instruction Tantra by its colophon on this page; the chapter before it, on the rejuvenation of the aged, རྒས་པ་གསོ་བ་བཅུད་ལེན, rgas pa gso ba bcud len, ends on etext p. 505). The passage names the causes of the failing and what is done for each. For cold wind, when the kidneys' heat fails, the channel's mouth cannot hold and what flows is mixed with water; remedies follow (donkey flesh and others, medicines in butter; the words are left untranslated, not read for sense here). For heat: pills with salt, given with beer. For poison: the outer, inner and secret signs are identified, it is killed by its antidote and the body is guarded. For a spirit (གདོན, gdon): in the dream (རྨི་ལམ, rmi lam) women lead him away. Its remedies: divination (མོ, mo), ཕྱྭ (phywa; the scan's glyph, see RD264 below; this passage's first reading was ཕྱུ, its sense not settled), the rite of service (རིམ་གྲོ, rim gro) and the dependent link (རྟེན་འབྲེལ, rten 'brel); in brackets, the healing by mantra: a thread twisted by a clean boy from wool of a sheep untouched by hands, seven vajra knots made in it, the mantra (in the Tibetan above, with the print's three dots; read by RD227, see below) spoken a thousand times over it, and the thread bound on; and in the other bracket, milk of cattle and goats (བ་ར, ba ra) mixed with dung (དྲི་ཆེན, dri chen) rubbed on the soles of the feet. The purpose (དགོས་པ, dgos pa): that one without illness, in his youth, may lie with women in every season without hindrance, quickly gladden his mind, beget a line of children, and go to every strong woman unhindered; it is said to be the highest of all attachment to sense pleasures. The colophon: the chapter on making the flavour (ro tsa), the ninety-first.

Read on the scan, 2026-11-09. The etext agrees with the scan in these lines but for the mantra, which it gives as ཨོཾ་ཧ་ར་ཧ་ར་བྷན་དྷ་ནི་ཤུ་ཏ་དྷ་རི་་་ནི་ཐ་ར་ཏེ་སྭཱ་ཧཱ (the line runs on to the next row, read by RD227 below). The print has small numerals above ll. 9–10, not transcribed. The print's page is the image minus 14.

RD227 (2026-11-09), by eye on crops. The mantra stands at the right-hand end of the row three above the foot of the printed bracket's close and runs onto the next row; crop x 1130–1870, y 1805–1880, 3×: ཨོཾ་ཧ་ར་ཧ་ར་བྷན་དྷ་ནི་ཤུ་ཏ་དྷ་རི followed by three dots at the margin (the print's own; the etext has them as «་་་»), and on the next row (x 240–1100, y 1925–1995, 2.5×) ནི་ཐ་ར་ཏེ་སྭཱ་ཧཱ་སྟོང་བཏབ་ལ་བཅིང་། The reading is the etext's, So the mantra is settled as the print gives it, with the print's own gap; its Sanskrit form is not needed and is not guessed. The word བ་ར (crop x 1180–1870, y 1585–1665, 3×) reads «བ་རའི་འོ་མར་དྲི་ཆེན་སྦྱར་བ་རྐང་མཐིལ་ལ་བྱུག» letter by letter clear. བ་ར (ba ra) is "cattle and goats" (བ་ཕྱུགས་དང་ར་, Tshig mdzod chen mo and others; also the cattle's pen), so "milk of cattle and goats", mixed with dung (དྲི་ཆེན, dri chen: "dung, ordure", one of the five nectars in Dan Martin's gloss); the earlier "unidentified" is dropped. ཕྱུ (phyu) in «མོ་ཕྱུ་རིམ་གྲོ་རྟེན་འབྲེལ» stays unsettled.

RD264 (2026-11-09), the same word on the Serthang scan at 10× (img. 507, x 790–960, y 1570–1660) and on the Bhutan scan: **the syllable is ཕྱྭ, not ཕྱུ.** On the Serthang scan the stack is ཕ with the ya-tag at its right and, under it, the small closed triangle of the wa-zur (ྭ), a separate mark from the open hook of ུ; the line reads «མོ་ཕྱྭ་རིམ་གྲོ་རྟེན་འབྲེལ». RD227's ཕྱུ (and the etext's ཕྱུ) was a misreading of this mark. So the Serthang, the Drathang (machine; Yigdzin-1) and the Bhutan (img. 418, mnb:p418, by eye) prints agree on «མོ་ཕྱྭ་རིམ་གྲོ». The letters are settled; what ཕྱྭ (phywa) means in the pair with མོ (mo, divination) is not looked up here (no dictionary was to hand: Steinert's clone is not on this machine) and no sense is claimed; the ch. 91 line stays unread for sense beyond the other three remedies, the rite of service (རིམ་གྲོ, rim gro), the dependent link (རྟེན་འབྲེལ, rten 'brel) and the mantra.
 The Drathang print (GT, img. 647, Yigdzin-1 only) has the root verse without either bracket: «གདོན་ཡིན་རྨི་ལམ་བུད་མེད་འདྲེན་པར་འགྱུར། །མོ་ཕྱྭ་རིམ་གྲོ་རྟེན་འབྲེལ་སྔགས་ཀྱིས་གསོ། །དགོས་པ་ནད་མེད་ལང་…», so the two bracketed remedies (the thread, the milk) are notes put into the Serthang text (whether they are interlinear notes in the Drathang print was not read: its folio is 1763 px wide), and the dream row's own verdict (a spirit; women leading away) is in the verse.

> Drikung dka' mchan (bdk, etext p. 817) and Khro ru (ktr, p. 684-685) are in st-dream-spirit-women (RD255).

Four commentaries on the line (RD304; etext only). The Blue Beryl takes the line as: if it is a spirit, the sign that does not change is a dream of clinging to women and having the seed drawn out; for that, do the divination with its omen (མོ་ཕྱྭ, mo phywa, ཕྱྭ spelled with the wa-zur here), identify the spirit, then do the rite of service and the dependent link that suit it; the dependent link is the milk with dung rubbed on the soles; the mantra healing is the thread, as the Serthang bracket has it. It then adds, beyond the bracket, a second mantra for the thread's seeds mixed with raw sugar and eaten, and a protection against a spirit's draining of the body's essence (human brain, white mustard, black guggul and womb-blood in equal parts, ground in honey and smeared on a cord bound round the waist): so the Blue Beryl's gloss is the source of the bracket's matter and goes further. Skyem pa Tshe dbang's gloss is shorter: divination (མོ་ལྟ, mo lta, not ཕྱྭ), identify the spirit, the rite and the link, and the thread; it has neither the milk nor the dung. Neither glosses ཕྱྭ, བ་ར or the milk's purpose. དྲི་ཆེན (dri chen) is in the commentaries as plain dung: the Blue Beryl and the Drikung commentary use «རང་གི་དྲི་ཆེན་དང་དྲི་ཆུ» (one's own dung and urine) and «ནད་པ་རང་གིས་འཁྲུས་པའི་དྲི་ཆེན» (the dung the patient himself has voided) in other chapters (the Blue Beryl's closing chapter; the Drikung commentary on a fever remedy), so it is plain dung and not a drug name there. བ་ར (ba ra) is in the Mes po'i zhal lung and Skyem pa in the phrase «བ་རའི་འོ་མ» among the causes of cold kapha (Mes po'i zhal lung p. 227, Skyem pa p. 638), beside curd, cold water and tea, so the milk is a milk of the class ba ra, as a food; no commentary defines ba ra. The Zur mkhar ba (I8LS19191) has no lines on ch. 91 (no hit for the lines' words); the Mes po'i zhal lung (I8LS19194) has only the phrase in the verse on the causes of cold kapha; Sde srid's Man ngag lhan thabs (lth) has neither line. The Drikung and Khro ru words are in st-dream-spirit-women.
