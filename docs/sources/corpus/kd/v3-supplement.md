> ལོ་ལའང་དཀྱུས་ལྡོག་རྣམ་པ་གཉིས། །དཀྱུས་མ་ཤིང་བྱི་ཤིང་གླང་དང༌། ། མེ་སྟག་ལ་་ སོགས་བུ་འདེད་ཀྱིས། །རིམ་བཞིན་ཆུ་ཕག་བར་དུ་སྦྱར། ། ལྡོག་པ་ལོ་ཡི་སྐོར་འགོ་ནི། །སྐྱེས་ [p. 490] པ་བུ་ལྔའི) 'སྟག་ནས་སྦུབ། །བུད་མེད་མ་ལྔའི)་སྤྲེའུ་ནས། །གྱེན་དུ་བཀེན་ལ་ལོ་གྲངས བགྲང་། །དཔེར་ན་ཤིང་ཕོ་བྱི་བ་ལ། །ཕོ་ནི་མེ་སྟག་མོ་ཆུ་སྤྲེལ། །སྐོར་འགོ་བྱས་ནས་གྱེན ཐུར་བརྩི། །གང་བབས་དམིགས་བཟུང་འགྲེལ་པ་ལྟ།

KD vol. 3, pp. 489–490 (etext pp. 509–510; etext only): in the first chapter of Dharmaśrī's *Moonbeams*, as KD prints it, how a year is turned in a person's reckoning.

A year has two ways of turning, the straight and the reversed. The straight: wood rat, wood ox, fire tiger and the rest, joined in order by the son-giving element to the water pig. The reversed turning of the year (ལོ་ཡི་སྐོར་འགོ, lo yi skor 'go): a man counts down (སྦུབ, sbub) from the tiger of the five sons, a woman counts up (བཀེན, bken) from the monkey of the five mothers, and counts the number of the years (ལོ་གྲངས, lo grangs). For example, for a wood male rat: the man's start is the fire tiger, the woman's the water monkey; having made the starting point, count up or down, and take where it falls as the commentary says.

The thirty starting points have a full, a middle and a short way of laying; here the full one: eight pebbles for each, 240 in all, settled. First the base of each. The fifteen Chinese: (1) the lord's reckoning, the white crystal garuḍa: from the wood rat-ox (own); (2) the spiritual friends, the white snow lioness: the fire tiger-hare (son); (3) the mantra-holder's, the black snake of existence: the earth dragon-snake (friend); (4) Shenrab's Bon reckoning, the turquoise dragon: the iron horse-sheep (enemy); (5) the great men and chiefs (མི་ཆེན་གཙོ་བོ, mi chen gtso bo), the earth elephant: the water monkey-bird (mother); (6) the young men, the striped tiger: the wood dog-pig (own); (7) the wives, the red copper ogress: the fire rat-ox (son); (8) house, field and ground, the temple with the garuḍa head: the water dragon-snake (mother); (9) the ground and the house's door: the wood horse-sheep (own); (10) armour, the wish-fulfilling jewel: the fire monkey-bird (son); (11) the child's tree and spring: the earth dog-pig (friend); (12) the god who helps: the iron rat-ox (enemy); (13) the harming spirit, the black grave bird: the water tiger-hare (mother); (14) the old, the deer: the wood dragon-snake (own); (15) the sick: the fire horse-sheep (son). The fifteen Tibetan begin with (1) the young children, the golden vajra: the earth tiger-hare (friend); (2) the girls: the iron dragon-snake (enemy); (3) the retinue and servants: the water horse-sheep (mother); (4) the horse: the wood monkey-bird (own); and so on to (15) the great men, the learned and the craftsmen (མི་ཆེན་ཡོན་ཏན་ཅན་དང་བཟོ་རིག་པ, mi chen yon tan can dang bzo rig pa), the luck reckoning, the eight-petalled lotus: the water dog-pig (mother). Having so fixed the base, the pebbles are laid: for each, first fix the base of the year of the one reckoned and of the starting point; then, by the difference of male and female year, from the base's male or female year count four up and four down, likewise seven up and seven down, and eleven up and eleven down; at the place counted up a white pebble, at the place counted down a black one. The fourth up and down fight the life force of the one reckoned with his life force, the seventh up and down his body with his body, the eleventh up and down his power with his power, and the base's own two luck-horses (the male and female year) his luck with his luck. The good and bad, the verdicts, are fixed by the commentary *The Magic Sieve*.

The eight: whether the bride's power-mountain is lost to the fire of existence; whether her direction is reversed; whether the lustre of gold and turquoise fades; whether the silk skirt frays; whether the father's line's back-mountain is good; whether the house of glory is pitched where she lives; whether the food of glory is lost to the enemy; whether the many mouths talk of emptiness. Second, white: her direction does not strike the nose of the *bstan ma* (བསྟན་མ, bstan ma), good. Black: it strikes the *bstan ma*'s nose, bad for the bride; strive at the service rite and the gto. Every check names the bride (བག་མ, bag ma) and none the groom.

> བདུན་པ་གསོན་རྡེལ་དཀར་བ དང་། །གཤིན་རྡེལ་ནག་ན་འཕུང་སྲི་གནོད། །འཕུང་སྲི་སྐུས་འདེབས་ལ་སོགས་བྱ། །རྡེལ་ ཁྲ་ས་བདག་འཁྲུག་གཏོ་བྱ། །ཐམས་ཅད་ནག་ན་རུ་ཌ་ཡི། །གཏོ་ནག་མགོན་པོ་གཡུལ བཟློག་བྱ། །བརྒྱུད་དཀར་དུར་ཁ་མི་བྱེ་ཞིང་། །ནག་ན་བྱེ་བས་འཕུང་སྐྱས་འདེབས། ། བཅུ་གཅིག་ས་བདག་མགོ་གསུམ་པའི། ཁ་ནས་གཏོ་ཆོག་དཀར་ནག་ཁྲའི། །རྩིས་ལ་་ བརྒྱད་དེ་གཏོ་དཀར་གྱིས། །ས་བདག་མཆོད་དམ་མི་མཆོད་དང་། ཁྲ་བོས་ས་བདག་ འཁྲུགས་མ་འཁྲུགས། །ནག་པོས་རུ་ཏྲ་བསྒྲལ་མ་བསྒྲལ། །མཚོན་སྒྲས་དགྲ་བོ་བཏུལ བཏུལ། །མི་ཁ་གྲེང་གཏོ་བཏང་མ་བཏང་། ། ཕོ་བརྒྱུད་ཁྲམ་བཟློག་བྱས་མ་བྱས། ། མོ་བརྒྱུད་ ཐུན་བཟློག་བྱས་མ་བྱས། ། ས་བདག་འགྲོ་ལམ་བཅོས་མ་བཅོས།

KD vol. 3, p. 541 (etext p. 561; etext only): the seventh check of the grave site (WB's twenty-fifth starting point), the two verdicts on the whole board that follow it, and the opening of the twenty-sixth starting point, the three-headed earth lord, whose eight checks the *Garland* reads as the rites white, black and speckled.

Seventh: if the living's pebble is white and the dead's pebble black, the ruin sri (འཕུང་སྲི, 'phung sri) harms; do its laying-down rite and the like. «རྡེལ་ཁྲ»: do the rite for disturbed earth lords. If all are black: Rudra's black rite, turning back the Protector's war. Eighth white: the grave is not opened; black: opened, lay the ruin's skyas 'debs. Then the eleventh [of the *Garland*'s fifteen], from the mouth of the three-headed earth lord: the rite sequence of white, black and speckled; the eight checks: the white rite, whether the earth lord is honoured or not; the speckled, whether the earth lord is disturbed or not; the black, whether Rudra is slain or not; the weapon-sound that tames enemies; the gossip rite; the male line's «ཁྲམ་བཟློག» and the female line's «ཐུན་བཟློག»; the road of the earth lord mended or not. One white pebble of the eight: the earth lord king is honoured, good.

## <a id="kd-3-p545-546"></a>kd.3:p545-546 The Garland: the general board's close, black in the great man's, the Bon priest's and the minister's place

*Read: etext, 2026-11-01.*

> རྡེ་འུ་ཐམས་ ཅད་ཁྲ་ན་བཟང་། །ཡོན་ཏན་མཁན་དང་མི་ཆེན་སར། །རྡེལ་ནག་གྱུར་ན་རླུང་རྟ་བསྐྱེད། ། བོན་སར་ནག་ནི་གསས་མཁར་བརྩིག །ཞང་བློན་སར་ནག་སྲོག་མཁར་ཚུགས། ། སྲོག་ [p. 546] བསྐྱེད་ཆོ་ག་དག་ལ་འབད། །བུད་མེད་ནག་ན་དབང་ཐང་བསྐྱེད། །བུ་ཚའི་ལུས་དང་བུ་མོ་ དཔལ། །བསྐྱེད་སོགས་རིགས་པའི་འཕྲུལ་འཁོར་བསྐྱེད།

KD vol. 3, pp. 545–546 (etext VEIE0OPI2F246A26_I4717 pp. 565–566, printed page = etext page − 20; BDRC's etext only, the lent scan not opened; «་་་» marks the etext's gaps, left out). The close of the general board of eight in Kun bzang blo gros's (ཀུན་བཟང་བློ་གྲོས, kun bzang blo gros) *Garland of Stars* (རྒྱུ་སྐར་ཕྲེང་བ, rgyu skar phreng ba), its verses nearly those of WB vol. 1, pp. 471–472.

When all the pebbles are speckled it is good. In the place of the learned (ཡོན་ཏན་མཁན, yon tan mkhan) and the great man (མི་ཆེན, mi chen): if the pebbles turn black, raise the luck (the etext writes «རླུང་རྟ», rlung rta, for the luck, klung rta). In the place of the Bon priest (བོན, bon): if black, build the gsas castle (གསས་མཁར, gsas mkhar). In the place of the uncle and minister (ཞང་བློན, zhang blon): if black, set up the life castle (སྲོག་མཁར, srog mkhar); the rite of raising the life force is the point. For women, if black, raise the power; for the sons, the body, and for the girls, the glory; raise them, and turn the magic wheel of intelligence for what is to be raised.

"… it matters to know the device of the starting points. Avoid the times when the planets and the fierce dates go about; do not send at the time the earth lords go about. … In avoiding and taking with bad earth lords: on a black day (ཉི་མ་ནག, nyi ma nag) she comes to widowhood; the poor day (དབུལ་ཉི, dbul nyi), ki kang, pi ling, zin phung, the fierce dates and the rest: of the earth lords that rise on the dates, flee the fierce and rough kind. "Of particular weight for a bride are the sky dog's (གནམ་ཁྱི, gnam khyi) own points, which are these. It is divided into seven dogs. The year's sky dog is the eighteen (བཅོ་བརྒྱད), and it is taught for the tiger, horse and dog three (སྟག་རྟ་ཁྱི་གསུམ); the month's sky dog is in the last autumn month (སྟོན་ཐ); the day's are again the tiger, horse and dog three, and the black sun (ཉི་མ་ནག་པོ) is a sky dog; the hour's sky dog is the evening time (སྲོད). Its own thing is the mewa seven (སྨེ་བདུན): when the mewa seven rises (ལྷག་གེ་ཤར་བ, the middle word not settled) and the bride, going out of the door, looks to the mouth of the north, she dies soon, there is no doubt. Besides, the tiger, horse and dog three in the year, month, day and hour are taught to bring a bad search for food, and earlier. The trigram's sky dog is Khen zon li (the trigrams); the planet's sky dog is Rāhu; the earth lord's sky dog is the Hal khyi (ཧལ་ཁྱི); flee the seven dogs at their piercing mouth (ཟུག་པའི་ཁ). Avoid the sky dog's food-search and examine in detail what to avoid and take in the sky's sendings. Besides, ordinary reckoners give the sky dog's body-parts, the results of its turning with the dates and the like: do not cling to such faulty ways of fools, but avoid and take by the intent of the pure text, by whichever of the new or old Chinese reckoning it is. Were one to reckon in detail, there are the twelve *khri sgo*, the *Kham yu*, the *Rog mo* and the rest of the pure text's teaching; here the gist is given in brief."

What it lays out: the sky dog is one danger told once under every one of the ten ways that the supplement lists (the year's, month's, day's, hour's, trigram's, planet's, earth lord's) and so runs across them, not above them. It is called of particular weight for a bride because one of its lines, the mewa seven's, says the bride dies soon if she goes out when it is up.

KD vol. 3, p. 644 (etext p. 664; etext only): in the *Garland*'s list of what is to be avoided in sending a bride, the verse naming the earth lords te se and te khyim, and the bstan ma.

The *te se* (ཐེ་སེ, the se) for the father's name (ཕ་མིང, pha ming), the *te khyim* (ཐེ་ཁྱིམ, the khyim) for the bride (བག, bag); the *bya ra ba* is bad for the husband; the *se byi* is bad for the bride's being led; the *hal khyi* is bad for all, so avoid it; the *bstan ma* is the sons' *bshan pa* (བཤན་པ, bshan pa, the one who slays them); the gold treasury and the turquoise treasury are the two to be wary of.
