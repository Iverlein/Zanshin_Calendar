## <a id="bvp-p662-665"></a>bvp:p662-665 Brahmavaivartapurāṇa, Kṛṣṇajanmakhaṇḍa 73, the Svapnādhyāya: the watches and the spans (vv. 5–6) that are the Svapnohana's

*Read: machine, 2026-11-09 (RD265).*

> स्वप्नास्तु प्रथमे यामे संवत्सरफलप्रदः ।
> द्वितीये चाष्टभिर्मासैस्त्रिभिर्मासैस्तृतीयके ॥ ५ ॥
> चतुर्थे चार्धमासेन स्वप्नः स्वात्मफलप्रदः ।
> दशाहे फलदः स्वप्नोऽप्यरुणोदयदर्शने ॥ ६ ॥
> प्रातःस्वप्नश्च फलदस्तत्क्षणं यदि बोधितः ।

*Brahmavaivartapurāṇa*, *Kṛṣṇajanmakhaṇḍa* ch. 73 (the running head of the page reads "73 a. ] Kṛṣṇajanmakhaṇḍam, 662"), the *Svapnādhyāya*, pp. 662–665 of Jivananda Vidyasagara's edition (vol. 2, 1888); verses 5–6 above (the 1888 layer and the 1935 Banaras print with Nepali agree, with OCR slips mended). The chapter is the answer of the Lord (Kṛṣṇa) to Nanda, who asks which dream gives what merit; it says that to hear the chapter gives the merit of a bath in the Ganges (v. 4).

**The spans, verses 5–6, in our words:** the first watch (*yāma*), a year; the second, eight months; the third, three months; the fourth, half a month (*ardhamāsa*); at the sight of dawn (*aruṇodaya-darśana*), ten days; a dream of the morning bears its fruit at that moment if one is woken. This is the whole series of the *Svapnohana* (svo:p268: a year, eight months, three months, half a month, ten days at dawn), and Negelein prints the same words under the *Svapnacintāmaṇi* (neg:p14-16, as his "b 5–6"; the layer's list of his letters is garbled at a–e, so that b is this chapter's text is likely, not read).

The next verses, in our words: a dream of what one thought of by day, or seen by one sick or anxious, is fruitless (7–8); so is the dream of the faint, the one pressed by urine, the terrified, the naked or loose-haired (9); one who has a good dream and sleeps again gets no fruit, even if he then tells it (10); telling a dream to a man of Kāśyapa's lineage, a poor man, a low man, an enemy, a fool, a woman, or at night brings harm (calamity, poverty, disease, fear, quarrel, loss of wealth, fear of thieves: 11–13); then the images, good (a cow, an elephant, a horse, a temple, a palace, a mountain, a tree; white garlands and clothes; a woman in white or yellow; gods, brahmans, cows, ancestors giving; sun, moon, fire; milk, curd, honey, sweets) and bad, ending with the ten names of the goddess to recite against a bad dream and a mantra of seventeen syllables (the 1935 print, v. 49–52).

No verse ties a dream to a *tithi*, a weekday, a nakṣatra, a month or a season; "white things are all praised, black things all blamed" (v. 37) is the only colour rule.
