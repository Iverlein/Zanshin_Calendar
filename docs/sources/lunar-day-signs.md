# The lunar day's animal, trigram and sme ba

Sigla and the rules for quoting are in [README.md](README.md). The Tibetan
page shows, for each lunar date, an animal, a trigram (*spar kha*) and a
number (*sme ba*), computed after Janson (E.9)–(E.11). This file records
what the texts say they mean as a day reading, read 2026-10-04.

**In short.** The trigram has a day reading: WB counts eight goddesses
through the month, and its exact count is the date's trigram under their
names; her day says what an illness that comes on then is traced to and
how it is treated (`Texts.TRIGRAM`). For the date's sme ba and animal,
neither WB nor KP gives a reading of their own: their readings are of the
person's progressed trigram and sme ba, of a sme ba counted from the
solstice; the date's animal is the *nyi ma* (open question 9, answered
2026-10-04), whose readings are WB's results of the twelve day animals
([day-animals.md](day-animals.md)) with KP's activity lists and, for funerals,
WB's earth lords of each animal day (below); KP ranks it last of the seven
factors of a day.

## The date's trigram and sme ba in the almanac

WB vol. 1, ch. 14 (the almanac), etext p. 188 (printed p. 178; from the
etext, not checked on the scan, as it only confirms what the app already
computes): after the day (*nyi ma*), write the trigram's letter and under it
the sme ba's number, which "rise on the dates" thus:

> སྤར་སྨེ་ཚེས་ལ་འཆར་ཚུལ་ནི། །སྟག་རྟ་ཁྱི་གསུམ་ལི་ཡིས་ཚེས། །ཕག་ལུག་ཡོས་གསུམ་ཟིན་གྱིས་ཚེས། །ཕྱི་འབྲུག་སྤྲེལ་གསུམ་ཁམ་གྱིས་ཚེས། །བྱ་གླང་སྦྲུལ་གསུམ་དྭ་ཡིས་ཚེས། །སྨེ་བ་ར་བཞིར་གཅིག་དཀར་ཚེས། །འབྲིང་བཞི་བཞི་ལྗང་མཐའ་བཞི་བདུན།

In tiger, horse and dog months the 1st is *li*; in pig, sheep and hare
months *zin*; in mouse, dragon and monkey months *kham*; in bird, ox and
snake months *dwa*. The sme ba of the 1st is 1-white in the first four
months, 4-green in the middle four, 7-red in the last four. Both are the
app's formulas (`TibetanCalendar.kt`: trigram from the month's animal and
the date, sme ba likewise).

## The trigram: the eight goddesses of the date

WB vol. 1, ch. 25 (divination of illness), pp. 449–450, img. 459–460.
Yigdzin-1 as base text, MITRA as witness (93% and 92% of syllables agree);
every difference in the passage settled on the scan, which BDRC holds only
at 691 px. Settled: ཕྱིན (l. 14, not བྱིན), བཙོ་བསྲེག (l. 17), བཙོག (l. 19),

མཆིན (l. 26); ཇོ་རངས (l. 12) is read as printed, for ཐོ་རངས "dawn".

> ལྷ་མོ་བརྒྱད་ཀྱི་རྩིས་བསྟན་པ། །འབྱུང་བ་ལྔ་ཡི་ཕུང་པོ་ལ། །འདུ་བ་འཁྲུགས་དང་འབྱུང་བ་རྒུད། །རྣམ་རྟོག་གདོན་གྱིས་ཉེན་གྱུར་ལ། །གློ་བུར་འཕྲལ་དུ་ན་གྱུར་ན། །མིག་དང་རྣ་བ་སྣ་དང་ལྕེ། །ལུས་དང་ལག་པ་མཚན་མ་རྐང་། །འབྱུང་ངོ་ཅོག་གི་ཟླ་བ་ལ། །ལྷ་མོ་བརྒྱད་ཀྱི་དོན་བྱེད་དེ། །ཚེས་གཅིག་འོད་འབར་མ་ནས་བསྐོར། །བསྟན་མ་གཉིས་ལ་དཀར་གསལ་གསུམ། །མདངས་ལྡན་བཞི་ལ་ཆར་འབེབས་ལྔ། །གཡོ་མེད་དྲུག་ལ་འོད་འཆང་བདུན། །སྐྱོབ་བྱེད་བརྒྱད་ལ་འོད་འབར་དགུ། །དེ་ལས་བསྡེབས་ཏེ་རིམ་པས་བསྐོར།

When the elements of the body are disturbed and an illness comes on
suddenly, the eight goddesses act in every month: 'Od 'bar ma on the 1st,
Bstan ma the 2nd, Dkar gsal ma the 3rd, Mdangs ldan ma the 4th, Char 'bebs
ma the 5th, G.yo med ma the 6th, 'Od 'chang ma the 7th, Skyob byed ma the
8th, 'Od 'bar ma again the 9th, and so round.

**The spirit names and rites, looked up 2026-10-04** in the dictionaries
of Steinert's collection (see [weekdays.md](weekdays.md)): *gre bo* a kind
of male *gdon* (Ives Waldo, Jim Valby); *rgyal 'gong* spirits born of the
*rgyal po* and the *'gong po* (Jim Valby); *dkor bdag* the spirit who
guards religious property, Pe har for one (Jim Valby); *dam sri* the
*sri* who harm vows (Rangjung Yeshe); *gri btsan* a *btsan* (Jim Valby);
*bse rag* the ghost that eats the essence of food and wealth (Rangjung
Yeshe, Tshig mdzod chen mo); *shu thor* pustules (Ives Waldo). *bkar*
in the rites column is *uccāṭana*, driving off (Negi, Mahāvyutpatti
glossaries), as in གྲེ་མོ་བཀར and དམ་སྲི་བཀར. 'Od 'bar ma's ཆུ་གྲི་བཀར, printed so
(1996, vol. 1, p. 449, BDRC OCR), is less plain: the dictionaries know
*chu gri* only as a small knife, "a surgical implement for removing
tumours" (Dan Martin), and her illness is a swelling; it may also be a
misprint for ཆུ་གྲེ, the water *gre* of Char 'bebs ma's row (question 10 in
[open-questions.md](open-questions.md)). The app keeps the parallel
reading, a rite to drive off the *chu gri*. *gnam the* and *brje thod
gtong* are not in the dictionaries; they stay as printed.

**Question 10 answered (2026-10-05).** WB itself uses *gri* for a
violent death and the spirit it leaves, and *chu gri* for a death by
water, some twenty times in vols. 1 and 2 (etext). In the list of bad deaths,
«…བརྣངས་ནས་ཤི་བ་ཆུ་གྲིར་ཤི། །རྟ་གྲིར་ཤི་དང་ཐོག་གིས་བསད།» (vol. 1, img. 339:
died by water, died by a horse, killed by lightning); with its remedy,
«བརྒྱུད་ལ་ཆུ་གྲིར་ཤི་བ་ལྡང་། །ཆུ་གླུད་བཏང་ཞིང་ཆུ་སྲི་མནན།» (img. 415: one who died
by water rises in the lineage; send a water ransom, suppress the water
*sri*); the woman's death in childbirth is *bu gri* («བུད་མེད་བུ་གྲིར་འཆི»,
img. 381). And WB has the rite for that one, in the same form as
'Od 'bar ma's: «…དམ་སྲི་དགུ་སྐོར་དང་། །གཤིན་རྡོག་བུ་གྲི་བཀར་བར་བྱ།» (vol. 1,
p. 435, img. 445, read on the scan; the 1996 etext loses བུ་གྲི, the Ser
thang print's etext has it). So ཆུ་གྲི་བཀར is driving off the *chu gri*, the
spirit of one who died by water: no misprint, and not the surgeon's
knife. It suits her day, whose causes include work on water channels and
whose harm-doers include the *klu*. Found 2026-10-05 with BDRC's
full-text search, where the phrase itself occurs only in WB and its
copies (and *Bod kyi rtsis rig kun 'dus chen mo*).

**The deeper count is the trigram.** WB continues (img. 460, l. 1–15):
"more profound than this, the count": in tiger, horse and dog months the 1st
is 'Od 'bar ma as above; in pig, sheep and hare months 1, 9, 17, 25 are
'Od 'chang ma's; in bird, ox and snake months Dkar gsal ma's; in mouse,
dragon and monkey months Char 'bebs ma's; the others follow in the same
order ("the first six have four days each, the last two three"):

> ཞེས་ཀྱང་དེ་ལས་ཆེས་ཟབ་པའི། །བརྩི་ལུགས་སྟག་རྟ་ཁྱི་ཟླ་ལ། །ཚེས་གཅིག་འོད་འབར་མ་ལ་སོགས། །སྔོན་དུ་བཤད་པའི་ཇི་བཞིན་བྱ། །ཕག་ལུག་ཡོས་ཀྱི་ཟླ་བ་ལ། །གཅིག་དགུ་བཅུ་བདུན་ཉི་ཤུ་ལྔ། །འོད་འཆང་མ་ཡི་ཉི་མ་དང་། … །བྱ་གླང་སྦྲུལ་གྱི་ཟླ་བ་ལ། །གཅིག་དགུ་བཅྭ་བདུན་ཉི་ཤུ་ལྔ། །དཀར་གསལ་མ་ཡི་ཉི་མ་ཡིན། … །བྱི་འབྲུག་སྤྲེལ་གསུམ་ཟླ་བ་ལ། །གཅིག་དགུ་བཅུ་བདུན་ཉི་ཤུ་ལྔ། །ཆར་འབེབས་མ་ཡི་ཉི་མ་ཡིན།

These are the month triads and starting points of the date's trigram in
the almanac rule above, with the goddesses in Janson's order of the
trigrams: *li* 'Od 'bar ma, *khon* Bstan ma, *dwa* Dkar gsal ma, *khen*
Mdangs ldan ma, *kham* Char 'bebs ma, *gin* G.yo med ma, *zin* 'Od 'chang
ma, *zon* Skyob byed ma. `TrigramGoddessTest` checks every date of 2026–2027
against the app's trigram. The notes printed after WB in the 1996 edition
(vol. 2, p. 498, img. 506) say the same in words: the eight-goddess count
is by the date alone roughly, and exactly by the trigram rising on the
date, ལི་འོད་འབར། ཁོན་བརྟན་མ་སོགས.

The app shows her day's reading on the trigram row (`Texts.TRIGRAM`). It is
an illness reading, with no activity lists and no tone; it does not enter
the day in brief.

## The sme ba

No reading of the date's sme ba was found:

- **KP §12** (img. 85–86) treats the trigram and sme ba of the birth year
  (the eight places *lus chad*, *srog 'tsho* …) and says outright that the
  sme ba's results are not given there: ...སྨེ་བའི་འཁོར་ལོ་… །འབྲས་བུ་གཙོ་བས་སྐབས་འདིར་ནི། །མ་བརྗོད་ནག་རྩིས་རྒྱུད་ལས་ལོངས། ("not told here; take them from the nag rtsis texts"; Yigdzin reading).
- **WB vol. 1, pp. 431–433** (img. 441–443) gives, for each of the nine,
  the illness, spirits and rites when one falls ill "on" it
  (སྨེ་བའི་ནད་གདོན་ངོས་བཟུང་ནི། །གཅིག་དཀར་སྟེང་དུ་ན་གྱུར་ན …). The sme ba
  meant is the patient's: the passage goes on to set one's birth sme ba
  against the one fallen to (རང་གི་སྐྱེས་སྨེས་བབས་སྨེར་འཐབ), and the notes
  printed after WB (vol. 2, pp. 496–497, img. 504–505) read the illness from
  the *babs spar* and *babs sme* together with the day of falling ill
  (བབ་སྤར་ན་ཉིན་ཉི་མ་བབ་སྨེ་བཅས་ཀྱིས་ནད་གདོན་ངོས་བཟུང). The same holds for
  the trigram passage just before the goddesses (pp. 447–448, img.
  457–458, ལི་ཡི་ཐོག་ཏུ་ན་བ་ན …). Both belong to the progressed signs (T4).
- **WB vol. 2, ch. 31, pp. 192–195** (img. 200–203) names a deity on each
  of the nine and what to avoid in the direction where it stands, but its
  daily sme ba is counted from the solstice, one a day
  (…ཉི་ལྡོག་པའི་ཉིན། །སྨེ་བ་གཅིག་དཀར་བསྐྱེད་པར་འགྱུར། །དེ་ནས་ཉིན་རེ་སྨེ་བ་རེ།),
  not the date's sme ba of the almanac.
- **Berzin**, *Details of Tibetan Astrology 9*, lists the magic-square
  number and trigram among the further features printed for a date, with no
  reading; *Henning*'s symbolic details give none.

## The animal

No reading of the lunar date's animal of its own was found:

- **KP img. 13** ranks the factors of a day: Rāhu, the weekday, the
  mansion, the date, the karaṇa, the yoga, the *nyi ma*, "each stronger
  than the next" (see [weighing.md](weighing.md)); the *nyi ma*, the day's
  animal sign, is last.
- **WB vol. 2, ch. 31, pp. 222–225** (img. 230–233) places the earth lords
  of each animal day (བྱི་བའི་ཉི་མར་ཆང་རྫར་གནས … the beer jar on a mouse day,
  the granary on an ox day …) with what to avoid, mostly in funerals. The
  day there is the *nyi ma*, which WB's almanac chapter lists before and
  apart from the date's trigram and sme ba (དེ་ནས་ཉི་མ་གང་ཡིན་འདྲི། །དེ་རྗེས་སྤར་ཁའི་ཡི་གེ་དང་།);
  the date's animal has no rule there. These readings mean the lunar date's
  animal (question 9 in [open-questions.md](open-questions.md), answered
  2026-10-04 from WB's notes, vol. 2, pp. 493–494). They are funeral
  reckoning: the passage itself says to apply them to *gshin rtsis* only
  (p. 225, «འདི་ཉིད་གཤིན་རྩིས་ཁོ་ན་ལ། །སྦྱོར་»). Read and built on 2026-10-04:
  [earth-lords.md](earth-lords.md).
- WB uses the date's animal as the sign of the date in the divination of
  health (vitality and body against the birth year), which the app shows
  under "Your day" (SPEC §5.9).
