# Halves and thirds at p. 438 are shares of a split; the notes' three-way example falls under the most taken as chief

WB p. 438's rule «འབྱུང་བ་ཕྱེད་མ་ནད་གཉིས་འདོམ། །སུམ་ཆ་སུམ་གཉིས་གདོན་རྐྱེན་ནད། །དེ་མིན་གང་མང་གཙོ་བོར་བཟུང་།» (wb.1:p438; the Lhasa prints «གསུམ་ཆ», the same word) and the notes' example (wb.2:p497) of two wood, two fire and three iron among seven do not meet:

- The example is a split among three elements in which none holds half or two thirds: the notes call each share "a third", iron "a third and a little more", and give the illness to iron as the greater share. That is p. 438's third clause, "otherwise the most is taken as chief", not its second.
- WB's own instances a few lines on (wb.1:p438, the mixtures) use the shares of two elements, and name for them illnesses of the humours, not of spirits: water two thirds and earth a third, white phlegm quenching the stomach's heat; water a third and fire two thirds, phlegm and bile growing; fire and water half and half, "the two illnesses weighed", hard to treat, hot above and cold below (the first clause, applied); wood two thirds with earth and iron a third, a wind-phlegm illness, easy to treat (a split of three elements in which one holds two thirds).
- With five pebbles (wb.1:p435-436) no element holds exactly a half or a third; with seven neither. The shares are proportions read from the counts, not exact fractions.

So the rule of halves and thirds is for a split in which one or two elements carry the count, chiefly of two; where three or more share it without a half or two thirds, the greater share decides, as the notes' example shows. What "a third and two thirds: an illness of spirits and conditions" adds to the humoral illness WB itself names for such a split is not said: read beside the instances, it may mark that spirits and conditions join the illness, not that the illness is spirits' alone (q228).
