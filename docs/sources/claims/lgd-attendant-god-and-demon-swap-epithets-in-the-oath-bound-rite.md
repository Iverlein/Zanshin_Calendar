# The god who comes alongside and the demon born together swap epithets in the Rin chen gter mdzod's rite of killing: one person's attendant pair

The pair of LGD p. 970, «ལྷན་ཅིག་སྐྱེས་པའི་ལྷ» (lhan cig skyes pa'i lha, the god born together) and «མཉམ་པོར་འོང་བའི་བདུད» (mnyam por 'ong ba'i bdud, the demon who comes alongside), stands in the Rin chen gter mdzod vol. 38, in the oath-bound great man's rite of killing, with the two epithets exchanged (RD355, 2026-11-10; I1KG53 img. 656, the three lines at the middle of the page, 4-fold by eye, Yigdzin-1 and the etext agreeing on every syllable). The enemy visualised in a black triangle is, by plague, poison, blood and whirlwind, "parted from the god who comes alongside (མཉམ་པོར་འོང་བའི་ལྷ)", and "the demon born together (ལྷན་ཅིག་སྐྱེས་པའི་བདུད)" turns into a wrathful oath-bound messenger with a beast's fangs that draws out his life-heart. So in this rite the two are one person's attendants, a protecting god and a harming demon; the rite tears the first from the enemy and turns the second against him; and either epithet, «born together» or «comes alongside», is put on either being. Two more places say the same of the pair from the side of one's own body: a Karma Kagyu commentary on the daily prayers offers to «the co-born god and the demons who come alongside that are attached to oneself» (a BDRC snippet, the commentary on collections of ritual prayers, search-only etext), and a modern prose names a thing one has of one's own, «like the co-born god and the demon who comes alongside» (a snippet).

What this gives q805 and q908: the phrase is not tied to Chöd's definition. In the Rin chen gter mdzod's own rite it is a being with the person, interchangeable with the co-born demon, and it is dispatched or turned like any enemy's attendant; the Chöd gloss (lgd-mnyam-por-ong-ba-bdud-is-the-companion-demon-of-self-clinging) remains one use. LGD p. 970 names the two as separate causes of dreams, with no gloss, so whether its author meant the Chöd demon of self-clinging, or the person's attendant demon beside his attendant god, is not told by any text read; the second reading is the one the Treasury's own rite shows. The question stays open.
