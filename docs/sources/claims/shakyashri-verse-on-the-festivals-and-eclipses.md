# The verse credited to Śākyaśrī: the four great festivals a hundred million, a lunar eclipse seven koṭi, a solar eclipse a hundred thousand koṭi

A verse on the multiplied merit of the times goes under the name of the Kashmiri great paṇḍita Śākyaśrī (པཎ་ཆེན་ཤཱཀྱ་ཤྲཱི, paN chen shAkya shrI). 'Bri gung Chos kyi grags pa's notes on recitation quote it whole (ckg.13:p19, etext; the same text stands in the *Rin chen gter mdzod chen mo*): the difference between the four great festivals (དུས་ཆེན་བཞི, dus chen bzhi) and ordinary days is, by the *Sil bu* and the *Myang 'das*, a hundred million-fold (ས་ཡ་ཕྲག་བརྒྱ, sa ya phrag brgya); a solar eclipse is a hundred thousand koṭi-fold, a lunar eclipse seven koṭi-fold; an eclipse on one of the four festivals is higher still. Dbyangs can dga' ba'i blo gros credits the same paṇḍita with the solar eclipse's hundred thousand koṭi, the lunar eclipse's seven koṭi and the hundredfold of the ordinary good days (dbl.2:p54-55, scan).

What follows:

- The White Beryl's three numbers of ch. 9 are exactly those credited to Śākyaśrī, so its seven koṭi for the lunar eclipse is that verse's number and no slip. RCZ's hundred koṭi for the moon stands alone among the six texts; RCZ itself names Śākyaśrī only for the treatise that identified the festivals' months.
- The festivals' two numbers, a hundred million and a hundred koṭi (a thousand million), are both given on the authority of the same two scriptures: «སིལ་བུ་མྱང་འདས་ལས» in the verse, «ལུང་སིལ་བུ་དང་མྱང་འདས་སོགས་ལས» in the Fifth Dalai Lama's rite. The difference is one word, ས་ཡ (a million) against བྱེ་བ (ten million), before «ཕྲག་བརྒྱ». The hundred million has the older named witness (the verse, and Chag the translator's saying beside it in ckg.13:p19); the hundred koṭi has the Fifth Dalai Lama, Dbyangs can dga' ba'i blo gros, who works it out as a thousand million lumps of dough, and RCZ.
- The scripture's own sentence was not found: the minor section (ལུང་སིལ་བུ, lung sil bu; the Vinaya's minor matters by the usual sense of the name, not checked here) and the Nirvāṇa were not searched beyond the phrase.

By rank the White Beryl stands for the eclipses and the ordinary days; on the festivals it is silent (festivals.md), and the texts that speak are all rank 3. The passages ckg.13:p19 and bdc:i76 rest on etexts.
