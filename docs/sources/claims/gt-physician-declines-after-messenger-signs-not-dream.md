# In the Moonbeams the physician's refusal rests on the messenger, his own state and the road, never on a dream of his own

The Moonbeams on the Eight Limbs states the physician's rule three times, each with its gloss: the physician does not go to a bad messenger (pp. 145-146); he gives up the sick when the messenger and the like show unlucky signs, otherwise strives to cure (p. 152); he does not treat the sick when a great messenger is seen coming to call him in a place and hour like the illness (p. 147). The omens are the messenger's looks, the physician's own occupation at the hour (cutting, tearing down, the offering for the dead, the burnt offering, lying on the ground, weeping), the day and the road. Its dream chapter (pp. 154-161) is about the sick man's dreams and says that the dreamer does not live; the dawn dream appears there only for the fruit of a dream (pp. 160-161, claim st-night-rule-blue-beryl-moonbeams). It has no rule that the physician declines after a bad dream of his own. Byang pa, Rin spungs pa and the Blue Beryl at the places read have none either. So the Four Tantras' ch. 7 order (messenger, dream, bearing) and the rule that the physician does not go rest on the Eight Limbs and the Moonbeams for the messenger and the road; the dream verses are the sick man's (gt:i42-43). That the Four Tantras themselves have the physician decline after a dream stays as RD330 left it.
