# Gtsang sman's physician's dawn dream as the first examination rests on neither the Siddhasāra nor the Four Tantras' messenger chapter; the dawn rule and the figures echo them

Question q957 asked whether Gtsang sman's arrangement, the physician's own dawn dream as the first of thirteen examinations with the rule that the physician does not go after a bad dream, rests on the Four Tantras' chapter on the messenger and the examination of the sick or on the Siddhasāra's chapter 5. Read side by side (dream-rules-three-texts-compared), it rests on neither as a whole:

- **The physician's own dream is in neither older text.** In the Siddhasāra the dreams of chapter 5 are a person's, healthy or sick, and the physician's part is the signs of his setting out (sds:p75-77). In the Four Tantras the dream verses are the sick one's, and the physician appears as the one who sets out and meets signs on the road (gt:i41, gt:i42-43). No sentence of Byang pa pp. 204-232, Rin spungs pa pp. 227-240 or Khro ru pp. 296-330 puts a dream of the physician beside the messenger (Searched and not found).
- **The messenger comes first in both older texts** (the Siddhasāra after the dreams, the Four Tantras at the head of the chapter); Gtsang sman puts the physician's dream before the messenger comes, on the night before. That order is his own as far as these texts show.
- **The rule that the physician does not go and does not treat has its counterpart in the Four Tantras' tradition**: the Moonbeams' verses quoted by Byang pa (pp. 207-210) say not to go on the unlucky days and when the messenger's words or looks are unlucky, and not to treat the sick one. These are rules about the messenger, the day and the road, not about a dream. The Siddhasāra states no such rule.
- **The dawn.** Gtsang sman chooses the dawn for the dream. The Four Tantras' chapter has the night rule that a dream of the first part of the night is forgotten and gives no fruit, while the dawn dream, seen and clear, bears fruit (gt:i42-43). It is likely that Gtsang sman's dawn rests on this rule, applied to the physician's dream where the Tantra speaks of the sick one's; the text of gsm:p263-264 does not name its source, so this is an inference.
- **The good dream is a ride.** Gtsang sman's good dream (new clothes, the rising sun, a saddled horse ridden to a mountain top or the north-east, known well-dressed people, music, a feast, flowers, leafy trees) has figures that stand in the Siddhasāra's good dreams (a horse ridden, a mountain climbed, a tree with fruit, a garland, a great water crossed) and in the Four Tantras' good road signs (flowers, a banner, a horse, music, food and drink). Its bad dream (naked, falling into mud, weary, killing, weeping, quarrels, hail, the setting sun) has figures in the Four Tantras' bad dreams (naked, sunk in mud) and the Siddhasāra's (falling, being bound). The sharing of figures is as far as the texts go.

So q957 is answered in part: the arrangement and the physician's own dream rest on neither; the dawn rule and the figures of the dreams echo the Four Tantras and the Siddhasāra; the rule to decline has a counterpart in the Moonbeams' lines on the messenger. Which source Gtsang sman followed stays open: Gtsang sman is modern and may follow an oral instruction or a text not yet read.

The launcher's row named the Instruction Tantra's chapters on the messenger and on examining the sick. The messenger is in chapter 7 of the Explanatory Tantra (བཤད་རྒྱུད, bshad rgyud) as the Drathang print and the commentaries show; the Explanatory Tantra's chapter 25, on examining by tricks and deceits (ངན་གཡོ་སྐྱོན་བརྟག, ngan g.yo skyon brtag), is a different list (learning the illness's signs, probing the household, delaying, tracing earlier treatments, and the like, Khro ru pp. 445-452) and has no dream of the physician in it.
