# The rule of the watches in older Indian dream books (Wayman 1967); the Svapnohana not mentioned

Wayman's survey "Significance of Dreams in India and Tibet" (History of Religions 7.1, August 1967, pp. 1–12; a scan of the article on the Internet Archive, read whole; modern, copyrighted: facts only) shows that the Svapnohana's rule of the watches belongs to an older Indian frame.

The Indian frame, as Wayman reports it: the Atharva-Veda tradition holds that a dream of the first watch of the night gives its fruit within a year, one of the second watch in six months, and one of the third watch is already half realised; the *Kālaprakāśikā*, which Wayman calls much later and which divides the night in four, gives a year, six months, a month, twelve days for dreams before dawn and a day for those before sunrise; and the Jaina *Aṅgavijjā* asks besides in which phase of the moon, waxing or waning, and in which part of the phase the dream came. The Svapnohana's own spans (a year, eight months, three months, half a month, ten days at dawn: svo:p268) follow the *Kālaprakāśikā* in kind (four watches and a dawn, the span shrinking from a year) but not in its numbers, and, unlike the *Aṅgavijjā*, tie nothing to the moon (the result of RD207 stands).

Wayman names Jagaddeva's *Svapnacintāmaṇi* (twelfth century, with a German translation, Negelein 1912), the short Sanskrit *Svapnādhyāya* and a Hindi *Svapnavicāra* as the Indian books devoted wholly to dreams, and says that in Tibet, besides brief passages in biographies, tantras and works on medicine and astrology, the dream (rmi lam) is classified in the appendix of the Gesar epic (good and bad dream omens, one page each). He does not mention the Svapnohana, the Tengyur or Vibhūticandra.

Not drawn from this: any statement of how Tibetan readers used the Svapnohana. The Indian numbers are quoted by Wayman from translations (Iyer 1917 for the *Kālaprakāśikā*) and from the Atharva-Veda appendix, and were not checked in the originals.
