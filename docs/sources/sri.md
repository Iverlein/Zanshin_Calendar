# The *sri*

Sigla and the rules for quoting are in [README.md](README.md). The app
names the *sri* only in activity wordings: *sri gnon* "suppressing the
*sri*" (KP box 50, the mansion and date readings, WB ch. 34), *gtad* and
*sri* rites (date 22) and *sri* rites against enemies (a mansion verse).
This file gathers what the sources say the *sri* are, so that their name
in English and Russian can be chosen (the owner, 2026-10-08: "vampire" is
the wrong word). It is knowledge about the spirits, not calendar logic, so
sources beyond WB stand here (the owner, 2026-10-08); WB's passages below
are from BDRC's etext of the 1996 edition (vol. 1 printed page = etext
page − 10, vol. 2 − 8) and **not yet read on the scan**.

## The word

- **Dictionaries** (Steinert's collection, 2026-10-08). *Dag tshig gsar
  bsgrigs*: (1) a harmful spirit, or the way of dying in a family line at
  set ages before the full span of life; (2) the name of a misfortune or
  obstacle that comes at a set time. *Dung dkar*: a class of *gdon 'dre*,
  or a misfortune or obstacle at a set time. *Brda dkrol gser gyi me long*
  (archaic words): a class of *gdon 'dre* whose way is to be eaten by
  Yama's Ender. Tsepak Rigdzin: a malignant spirit born of a dead person
  in the place where they lived, making trouble around it; also a
  disastrous period of shared karma, accidents following one another in
  one place. Ives Waldo: a kind of *gdon*; a disaster or obstacle with a
  fixed measure of time. Rangjung Yeshe writes "si spirit"; Berzin's
  glossary has no entry.
- **Ramble** (Charles Ramble, *Introduction to the Dus sri*, Text, Art and
  Performance in Bon Ritual, Vienna, cirdis-archive.univie.ac.at, read
  2026-10-08): among the most complex of the autochthonous Tibetan
  spirits; the word is cognate with *srid pa*, "to come into existence",
  and a text calls the *sri* its opposite, the nadir against the zenith;
  *sri* and *sri'u* also mean "child", and texts leave open whether the
  *sri* prey on children or take their form (*sri can*, "having a *sri*").
- **Common to all**: the *sri* is known by repetition. Deaths or losses of
  the same kind come again at intervals, in one family or place, and the
  *sri* is named after what it takes.

## How they come to be

- **At the beginning of the world** (Bon): the fourteenth-century *gZi
  brjid* counts ten kinds, which emerge during the cosmogony from the
  residue of the Grey Egg of Lightlessness (Ramble).
- **The first *sri*** (Bon, the *dus sri* rite Ramble edits): a girl
  rejected by her father at birth, bound in a compact by Gshen rab, her
  cousin, and tricked into being buried alive, though innocent; she is
  later called on to subdue a second generation of *sri*, who kill the
  children born to a young princess one after another and are the
  princess's own closest female relatives (Ramble, announcement of his
  reading course *Lektüre II*, CIRDIS, University of Vienna, PDF, read
  2026-10-08).
- **From a broken vow** (Buddhist): the *dam sri* is one who broke the
  pledge (*dam tshig*) with their lama in an earlier life and is born now
  as a human or a spirit (*Dung dkar*); nine sibling spirits who broke
  their pledges (Rangjung Yeshe).
- **From particular deaths** (WB's death reckoning, vol. 1, pp. 426–427,
  etext): *sri can* arise from one who killed a child of an illicit union
  (*nal bu bsad pa'i sri can*), from a woman who died pregnant (*snel
  sbrum shi ba'i sri can*), and with uncleanness after such births.

WB's household reckoning (vol. 1, pp. 305–306, etext) seats one in "the
row" (*gral na*) of each of the house's gods: *byur sri* with the mother
god, *zhing sri* with the uncle god, *dgra sri* with the war god, *pho
sri* with the male god, *thab sri* with the hearth god, *sgo sri* with the
door god, *god sri* with the wealth god. Its remedy closes the passage:
«འདྲེ་ལ་གྲངས་གདབ་སྲི་མནན་བྱ། … སྲི་རུ་ཐོད་དགུ་རུ་བཅུ་གནོན།», for the
demons a ransom, the *sri* pressed down, under nine skulls and ten horns.

## The rites that avert them

- ***sri gnon*, *sri mnan*, pressing down the *sri*.** "A rite that hides
  and presses down the harmful spirit's effigy in a three-cornered pit"
  (Tshig mdzod chen mo, also Ives Waldo); Tsepak Rigdzin adds capture,
  burial and burning. Its days are what the app's lists name.
- **The rite in practice** (Ramble, *Vampire subjugation, Kag 2010*,
  kalpa-bon.com, read 2026-10-08): a Bon lama of Lubrak in Mustang,
  called after the related men of a family died a few years apart; the
  agent was found by divination to be a *dus sri*. An effigy (*linga*)
  with a cavity of red butter, "a substitute for blood", goes into a
  triangular pit of black clay set on black sand marked with a six-pointed
  star; nine barberry firebrands (*shing 'gal ba dgu*), which a Bon myth
  calls the weapon against the *sri*, stand round it. The *sri* is
  summoned into the room, the effigy cut, stabbed with the *phur ba* and
  shot with arrows, then sealed with everything else in a yak horn, and
  buried at a meeting of three roads, the digger facing the direction the
  earth spirit's sleeping position allows; a stone, thorns and a fire go
  on top. For the *sri* of small children a dog's skull is used instead
  of the horn.
- ***sri bzlog***, turning back the *sri* (Ives Waldo); *sri bzlog 'khor lo
  'bar ba*, a text recited after an infant's death so that the *sri* do
  not take other children (Rangjung Yeshe).

## The call of the grave

The owner (2026-10-08) pointed to the East Asian idea that a grave or a
burial calls the next death in a family, as the Korean film *Exhuma*
(파묘, 2024) opens with. WB's death reckoning has the same idea in its
own terms, and the almanacs of Japan and China keep it as days.

- **WB: the death-mouth** (*shi kha*; vol. 2, pp. 110–111, etext). A death
  on certain combinations of trigram (*spar kha*) and year leaves the
  dead person's "death-mouth" open: «ས་ལ་སྦས་ན་རོ་ཁ་ཕྱེ», buried in the
  earth, the corpse's mouth opens; on the *khen* trigram «དུར་སྒོ་གནམ་ལས་བྱེད»,
  the grave's door is opened by the sky; left untreated it is "bad for
  seven", or "three people die". Each case has its rite of "cutting the
  death-mouth" (*shi kha bcad pa*): the names of the dead person and of
  the demon written in an animal's skull (a horse's, a dog's, a sheep's,
  a donkey's) and pressed down under or on the corpse, sometimes a
  *stūpa* built over it. And for deaths that repeat: «ཡང་ཡང་ཤི་ན་འདི་ལྟར་བྱ»,
  "if people die again and again, do this", among the signs that *'phung
  sri* has risen: the rite ends
  «སྲི་མནན་སྦྱིན་སྲེག་ཤི་ཁ་ཆོད», the *sri* pressed down, a fire offering,
  and the death-mouth is cut. Here the *sri* is the agent of the
  grave's call. A study of these passages: 曾德明 (Tseng Te-ming),
  西藏文化中的死亡預測與對治 *Tibetan Death Divinations and Rituals*,
  臺灣宗教研究 7:2 (2008), pp. 77–92, on WB and the *Bkra shis 'dod 'jo*
  (abstract read 2026-10-08; the article not seen).
- **Korea: *myot-baram*** (묫바람, "grave wind"): harm to the descendants
  from an ancestor's grave, illness, early death, misfortune (GQ Korea's
  preview of *Exhuma*, 2024); in the film a shaman finds it behind a
  family's illness and the grave is opened and moved.
- **Japan.** The app's 旧暦 page already has two such days: 友引, when a
  funeral is avoided lest it "draw a friend" after the dead (folk belief,
  こよみ博物館), and 重日, the 巳 and 亥 days, on which good and bad
  alike repeat, so that weddings and funerals are avoided; 復日 repeats
  "like 重日" (国立国会図書館, 日本の暦, 吉凶を表す言葉③下段, read
  2026-10-08; the app shows 復日 with weddings avoided).
- **China.** Almanacs (通書) mark days to avoid a burial lest another
  death follow; no source for their rule was found in this search
  (重喪日 is the name given in secondary accounts), so nothing about them
  is stated here.

So the *sri*, in WB's own reckoning, is what makes the dead call the
living: the same loss returning in one family. An English name close to
the owner's "call of the grave" is a description, not a translation;
none of the sources above uses one word for it.

## Their name in the app

**Decided by the owner (2026-10-08): "harmful spirits of the dead"**,
Russian «вредоносные духи умерших», with *sri* kept in brackets as the app
keeps *bla*. The reason: the *dam sri* are those who broke their pledge
(*Dung dkar*: one who broke the *dam tshig* with their lama in an earlier
life, born now as a human or a spirit), and WB's death reckoning makes the
*sri* the agent of the dead person's open "death-mouth"; both agree with
the Japanese and Korean view of the dead calling the living (above).
"Vampire", the scholars' word, is not used.

The wordings keep their sense and change only the term: "suppressing
harmful spirits of the dead (sri)" (*sri gnon*, *sri mnan*), "gtad rites
and rites of the harmful spirits of the dead (sri)" (date 22, «གཏད་སྲི»),
"rites of the harmful spirits of the dead (sri) against enemies" (Ārdrā,
«དགྲ་ཡི་སྲི་སྒྲུབ་པ»); the trigram *zon*'s reading names the *dam sri* as
"harmful spirits of the dead who broke their pledges".
