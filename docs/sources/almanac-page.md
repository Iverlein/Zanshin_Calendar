## Where WB says it

The work plan had pp. 171–178. Those pages hold three things:

- pp. 171–173 (img. 181–183): the end of the model almanac (Hor month 10,
  read for T2.10, [tibetastromed.md](tibetastromed.md)), then the verse
  that closes chapter 13, which lists what an almanac's day line (*tshes
  kyi gtan le*) holds, p. 173 (img. 183).
- pp. 174–179 (img. 184–189): chapter 14, «ལེའུ་ཐོ་རྒྱས་འབྲིང་བསྡུས་གསུམ་སྦྱོར་ཚུལ», how the
  three almanacs are made: the full one (*rgyas pa*), the middle one and
  the short one. Of the full almanac: the year's front matter (pp. 174–176),
  the month's heading (p. 176) and the day's entries in their order
  («འགྲོལ་བའི་གོ་རིམས་འདི་ལྟ་སྟེ», pp. 177–178); then the middle and short
  almanacs (pp. 178–179), which write less of the same.
- The chapter ends on p. 179 (img. 189).

The year's front matter (verses of homage, the year's names in Indian,
Tibetan and Chinese, its element, sme ba and planets, the wheels of the
*dbyangs 'char*, the year's results from the *Mdo sde gsang ba* to the
*Kar myang* reckoning, the earth lords' charts) and the middle and short
almanacs are not day entries and are not inventoried here.

**The mansion's change** (p. 178, entry 18): «དེ་ཡང་ཉ་སྟོང་རི་མོ་ཡི། །བར་ནས་སྐར་མའི་འཕོ་མཚམས་རྣམས། །འོལ་ཚོད་མུན་མདའ་འཕེན་པ་ལས། །རྟོགས་དཀའ་རགས་ཙམ་སྐད་ཅིག་ལ། །ཤེས་འདོད་སྔོན་མའི་དལ་མྱུར་གྱིས། །རྗེས་མའི་དལ་མྱུར་ཕྲི་བའི་ལྷག །ཚེས་ཀྱིས་བགོས་ནོར་ཞག་རེ་ཡི། །ཡུལ་གྱིས་སྤྱོད་པའི་ལོངས་སྤྱོད་དེ། །སྔོན་མའི་སྟེང་དུ་ཙམ་བསྣན་པའི། །གྲངས་ཀྱིས་སྐར་གནས་ལོངས་ན་འཕོ། །དེ་ནི་དལ་མྱུར་རྐང་པ་ནི། །མ་འཕོས་དུས་སུ་རྣམ་དག་དང་། །འཕོས་ཚེ་ཆུ་ཚོད་མན་རགས་པ།»
The changes of mansion, "hard to grasp as shooting an arrow in the
dark", are known roughly by subtracting one row's figure (*dal myur*, the
moon's place) from the next: the remainder is the day's course; added in
proportion to the earlier figure, the moon changes mansion when the sum
fills the mansion. Exact while the mansion holds, rough "in chu tshod and
below" once it changes. The figure is the moon at daybreak, which is
Henning's second figure (henning-phugpa.tsv). Entry 5 writes the second
mansion when it comes in daytime; the app takes daytime by WB's own day
length (ch. 15, below), counted from daybreak. A fast
moon can enter two mansions in one day (1 November 2026).

**The skipped yoga** (entry 8) is reckoned the same way on the yoga's sum
(the moon at daybreak with the true sun, Henning's fourth figure): a yoga
that begins and ends between two daybreaks is written with the day's own,
with both times.

**Viṣṭi's span** (p. 177, entry 9): «དེ་ནས་བིཥྚིའི་འཕོ་མཚམས་ནི། །མཚོ་དྲག་སྐྱོན་དང་དེ་ཉིད་ཕྱི། །ཀླུ་ཚེས་ལག་མིག་བུག་ཟུང་སྔ། །དེ་ཕྱིར་སྔ་ཆས་ཚེས་སྔ་མར། །ཤས་ཆེར་ཕྱིན་མང་ཕྱི་ཆས་ཀྱང་། །སྔ་མར་བྱིན་པ་སྲིད་པ་ཡིན། །ཚེས་ལོངས་སྔོན་མས་མཁའ་རོ་ལ། །ཕྲི་ལྷག་ནམ་ལངས་གོང་དུ་འདས། །ལྷག་དང་ད་ལྟར་ལོངས་སྤྱོད་གཉིས། །བསྲེས་ལ་ཟུང་གིས་ཕྱེ་བའི་ནོར། །བིཥྚི་རྒྱུ་བའི་ཡུན་ཚད་ཡིན། །སྔ་ཕྱིར་འཆར་བར་ལོངས་སྤྱོད་དེ། །རིམ་བཞིན་ཚུགས་དང་རྫོགས་ནས་འཇུག»
The later half of the 4th, 11th, 18th and 25th (*mtsho* 4, *drag* 11,
*skyon* 18, *de nyid* 25), the earlier half of the 8th, 15th, 22nd and
29th (*klu* 8, *tshes* 15, *lag mig* 22, *bug zung* 29); "so the earlier
half mostly falls into the date before, and the later can too". The
date before's end subtracted from sixty (*mkha' ro*) is what passed
before daybreak; with the present date's end, halved (*zung*), it is
Viṣṭi's length; in the later half it begins halfway and ends with the
date, in the earlier it begins with the date. Both readers agree on these
lines. The app shows the span on each calendar day it touches.

the sun's course; *sgang* and *dbugs thob* are found the same way. The
step («ཆུ་ཚོད་ཡིད་བསྒྱུར་ཆུ་སྲང་རོས། །བགོས་པ་སྟེང་བྱིན», the same in the 1996

almanac manual, p. 315, has WB's own words with ༦ over *ro*; question 15). The time is given in whole chu tshod,
half a one counted as one («གཟའ་ཡི་ཆུ་ཚོད་ཕྱེད་ལོངས་ན། །གཅིག་སྟེར»; his «གཟའ་ཡི་ཆུ་སྲང་མཁའ་མེས་དོར»,
p. 224). The app follows the rule so (`DayTimes.termStep`). Worked by
hand on Henning's 2026 figures, the 1st month's *dbugs thob*: the sun
passes its measure 21;58 by 0;0,49 at the end of the 2nd date, 19;22 on
19 February; no chu tshod and 49 chu srang give 0 × 14 + 8, taken from
19 in whole chu tshod: 11 after daybreak, 09:24, as the app gives it
(`DayTimesTest`). The almanac writes the
term on the date it falls in, one that falls before daybreak on the
date before (p. 177).

The day lengths ch. 15 lists at each month's *sgang* (31;10 at the 3rd,
32;20 at the 4th, 33;30 at the 5th, the summer solstice, 30 at the 2nd
and 8th, 26;30 at the 11th, 27;40 at the 12th, 28;50 at the 1st) follow
p. 182's rule, 1;10 longer or shorter each sign-month («ཁྱིམ་ཟླ་རེར། །ཆུ་ཚོད་རེ་དང་ཆུ་སྲང་ཕྱོགས། །འཕེལ་འགྲིབ»):
the app reckons the day's length so, by the true sun
(`DayTimes.dayLength`, tested against each listed value), as the daytime
of entries 5 and 11; the sky line keeps the place's own sunrise and
sunset (SPEC §6).

Not built from ch. 15: the seasons' signs, the year's lord by
the sun's entry into Aries and the results of the sun in each sign
(p. 182), and the doubled months (p. 183); the black months of p. 183 are built (T2.21, [month-entries.md](month-entries.md)).

## The first date's trigram and sme ba

p. 178: «སྤར་སྨེ་ཚེས་ལ་འཆར་ཚུལ་ནི། །སྟག་རྟ་ཁྱི་གསུམ་ལི་ཡིས་ཚེས། །ཕག་ལུག་ཡོས་གསུམ་ཟིན་གྱིས་ཚེས། །བྱི་འབྲུག་སྤྲེལ་གསུམ་ཁམ་གྱིས་ཚེས། །བྱ་གླང་སྦྲུལ་གསུམ་དྭ་ཡིས་ཚེས། །སྨེ་བ་ར་བཞིར་གཅིག་དཀར་ཚེས། །འབྲིང་བཞི་བཞི་ལྗང་མཐའ་བཞི་བདུན། །དེ་ནས་རིམ་བཞིན་ཤེས་པར་བྱ།»

The month of the tiger, horse or dog begins on *li*, of the pig, sheep or
hare on *zin*, of the mouse, dragon or monkey on *kham*, of the bird, ox
or snake on *dwa*; the four first months of a season begin on the white
1, the four middle on the green 4, the four last on 7; then in order.
The app's formula (Janson, E.3) gives exactly this on every first date
of 2000–2049, **with the seasons of the Chinese reckoning** (the first
months 11, 2, 5, 8), not the Kālacakra's: a third passage, beside the
two of chapter 31 ([tibetastromed.md](tibetastromed.md)), where WB's
seasons in this kind of reckoning are the Chinese ones. `AlmanacPageTest`.
