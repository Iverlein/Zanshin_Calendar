# Festivals and monthly observances

Sigla and the rules for quoting are in [README.md](README.md). What the
festivals' and the monthly observances' readings rest on (SPEC §5.7,
§5.8; ROADMAP X5), read 2026-10-16. Rabten's and Henning's wording is
theirs: the readings state its facts in the project's own words.

## Edition Rabten, *Tibetan Calendar 2026*

The front matter (rabten.eu/downloads/calendarEN.pdf, 23 pages; read in
its text layer), in substance:

- The 8th, the full moon, the new moon and eclipse days are special days
  for any wholesome action; lay practitioners are especially recommended
  to take the Eight Vows on them (the readings: "the eight precepts").
- The 10th and the 25th are days for the *ganacakra* or tsok rites of
  tantra (ཚོགས, tshogs), especially recommended for guru pūjās.
- Sojong (Upoṣadha) days, twice a month, are for the confession of
  monastic communities (the full and the new moon in the calendar's
  pages).
- *Dharma festivals*: Chötrul Düchen, the day of the great miracles; Saga
  Dawa, the fourth month, on whose full moon the Buddha took birth,
  attained enlightenment and passed into parinirvāṇa; Chökhor Düchen, the
  first turning of the wheel; Lhabab Düchen, the return from the realm of
  the gods; Gaden Ngamchö, Tsongkhapa's parinirvāṇa; the masters'
  parinirvāṇa days and birthdays (not kept by the app). Of them: very
  special days for practice, on which any positive or negative action has
  a strongly multiplied effect.
- *Tibetan festivals*: Losar, the first day of the year, celebrated in
  every auspicious and joyous way; Zamling Chisang, the smoke offering
  (བསང, bsang) to all the protectors; Sangpo Chuzom, the ten good omens
  (not kept, SPEC §5.7).
- In the month pages: "Thanksgiving to Dharma-Protectors" on the 29th of
  the twelfth month (16 February 2026), the day before Losar.

## The White Beryl's month heading

WB vol. 1, p. 176 ([almanac-page.md](almanac-page.md), *The month's
heading*): «ཐུབ་དབང་ལྷུམས་ཞུགས་བལྟམས་པ་དང་། །ཆོས་འཁོར་ཆོ་འཕྲུལ་མྱ་ངན་འདས། །ལ་སོགས་དུས་ཆེན་བཅས་པ་རྣམས། །རང་ལུགས་གང་བཤད་བཞིན་འདྲི», the Sage's
entry into the womb, birth, turning of the wheel, miracles and passing,
and the other great occasions (དུས་ཆེན, dus chen), are written at the head
of their month as one's own tradition gives them. The festivals' family
note says this. WB's ch. 9 merit, «དུས་བཟང་དཀྱུས་རྣམས་བརྒྱ་འགྱུར» (p. 62), a
hundredfold on the ordinary good occasions, does not name the festivals,
so no reading uses it for them.

### What Nyingma communities publish, read in the browser on 2026-11-16

Facts only; the pages' wording is theirs.

  (rigpawiki.org/index.php?title=Four_major_Buddhist_holidays): the four

### What was read

- Himalayan Art Resources (HAR, Jeff Watt), *Shakyamuni Buddha: Life
  Story Main Page* (himalayanart.org/search/set.cfm?setid=558, updated
  2016–2017): the twelve deeds as painted in the life-story sets, with
  their Tibetan names, and the eleventh deed divided into the display of
  miracles, the Lotus Sutra narrative, the descent from Tushita and the
  miracles at Shravasti. The sets listed: nine-painting sets (Dege block
  prints, Tibet House, Choying Dorje and others), two-painting sets and a
  single-composition life story. Its page *Miracles at Shravasti*
  (setID=2902): fifteen miracles in fifteen days at Shravasti, painted
  within the life-story sets and at times as a set of their own. Its
  sub-set pages (346 Māyādevī, 1504 the miracles, 1505 the descent from
  Tushita, 1507 the parinirvāṇa) carry images and no text on the scenes.
- HAR, *Eight Auspicious Symbols* (setID=1178, updated 2022): the eight
  as parasol, golden fish, treasure vase, lotus, right-turning conch,
  glorious endless knot, victory banner, wheel; shown together or singly;
  also on initiation cards. English names only on the page.
- HAR, *Tsongkapa Iconography* (setID=4181, Jeff Watt 2019): the
  attributes of Tsongkhapa in painting are the yellow pandita hat,
  monastic dress, the teaching gesture, a sword and a book on flower
  blossoms, the seat in vajra posture; a separate form shows him
  descending from Tushita. No flame is named for the sword; no Tibetan
  names on the page.
- HAR, *Torma Offering Main Page* (setID=1062): the torma (གཏོར་མ, gtor ma,
  Sanskrit baliṃta) as a cone-shaped ritual food of barley flour, coloured
  and adorned with discs of butter; red triangular ones for fierce
  protectors such as Mahākāla and Śrī Devī; used among other things
  for turning back obstacles in the New Year celebrations; shown in
  paintings hung in the protectors' temples.
- Wikipedia (CC BY-SA, a secondary source; its sentences cite books not
  seen here): *Ashtamangala* gives the Tibetan names of the eight
  (the right-turning white conch དུང་དཀར་གཡས་འཁྱིལ་, dung dkar g.yas 'khyil;
  the endless knot དཔལ་བེའུ་, dpal be'u; the pair of golden fish གསེར་ཉ་,
  gser nya; the lotus པདྨ, pad ma; the jewelled parasol རིན་ཆེན་གདུགས་,
  rin chen gdugs; the treasure vase གཏེར་ཆེན་པོའི་བུམ་པ་, gter chen po'i
  bum pa; the wheel of Dharma ཆོས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ་, chos kyi 'khor lo; the
  victory banner རྒྱལ་མཚན་, rgyal mtshan) and says Tibetans use the set
  in household and public art. *Dharmachakra*: the wheel with deer is
  the Deer Park of Sarnath, an Indian pairing seen from south to north
  and still set on the roof ridges of Tibetan monasteries. *Jokhang*:
  on the roof and its ridges stand golden deer flanking a Dharma wheel,
  with victory banners and fish. *The Twin Miracle*: from the upper half
  of the Buddha's body fire, from the lower half streams of water, then
  alternated, in six colours; Tibetans keep it with Chötrul Düchen.
  *Chotrul Duchen*: also the Butter Lamp Festival; lamps shaped like
  deities, flowers and auspicious symbols, lit on the 15th. *Lhabab
  Duchen* (ལྷ་བབས་དུས་ཆེན, lha babs dus chen): the descent on a triple
  celestial ladder made by Indra and Brahmā, rainbows about it, landing at
  Sankassa; the three months of teaching Māyādevī before. *Maya*: in her
  dream a white elephant holding a white lotus circled her three times and
  entered her right side. *Losar*: the pre-Buddhist winter incense and
  smoke offerings to local spirits; houses decked with signs in flour
  (sun, moon, swastika) and juniper, cedar and rhododendron burnt as
  incense. *Torma*: gtor ma, from "to scatter", offering and letting go.
- Lotsawa House, Jigme Lingpa, *Praise of the Twelve Acts of the Buddha*
  (the Tibetan text, whose author's work is old; the English translation
  is under a non-commercial licence and was not used): the twelve acts
  in verse, from the descent from Tuṣita and Māyādevī's womb to the
  parinirvāṇa. It names no emblem for any of them. The Śrāvastī stanza
  speaks of the miracles (ཆོ་འཕྲུལ་, cho 'phrul) that silenced the
  tīrthika teachers, and the "four bases of miraculous powers" (རྫུ་འཕྲུལ་
  རྐང་པ་བཞི, rdzu 'phrul rkang pa bzhi).

### The eleven, one by one

Confirmed = a published page gives the thing as a Tibetan-Buddhist
emblem or as part of the festival's story. It is not confirmation that
the thing is painted as the frame's crown for that festival: no page read
says so.

| Festival | Proposed motif | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Losar | the eight auspicious symbols set round a gold band; the vase of treasure at the crown | The eight symbols and their Tibetan names confirmed (HAR 1178, Wikipedia); the treasure vase is one of the eight. No page links the eight to Losar; the pages tie Losar to smoke and incense, flour signs and a protector's torma. Held as proposed, the link unconfirmed. |
| Chötrul Düchen | the twin miracle, fire above and water below | Confirmed as the event's content (fire from the upper half, water from the lower, Wikipedia *The Twin Miracle*), and the miracles at Śrāvastī are painted in the life-story sets (HAR 2902). The festival's own folk emblem is the butter lamp (Wikipedia). Both stand; the aureole may carry the lamps instead if the owner prefers. |
| Kālacakra | rings in the colours of the elements | Not confirmed: the pages read give the mandala as palaces one within another (body, speech, mind, wisdom and bliss), not rings of elements by colour. HAR's Kālacakra pages (setID=167) give no such detail. Dropped for a plain aureole in the day's colours, unless a source is found. |
| Birth of the Buddha | flowering branches of Lumbinī, a lotus bud, seven small lotuses for the steps | Nothing read gives the branches, the bud or the seven lotuses. The birth is the third of the twelve deeds in HAR's list (Lumbini, sku bltams pa) and Jigme Lingpa's verse has Māyādevī giving birth in the Lumbinī grove, "a lotus opening in blossom" (Tibetan: ལྷུམས་ཀྱི་གེ་སར་བཞད་པ་ལུམྦིའི་ཚལ), a figure of speech. Dropped for a plain aureole. |
| Saga Dawa Düchen | the bodhi leaf, the vajra seat | The bodhi tree and the vajra seat appear in the twelve deeds (HAR: proceeding to the foot of the bodhi tree, byang chub snying por gshegs pa), but no page read says how they are painted. Dropped for a plain aureole. |
| Zamling Chisang | juniper smoke over a burner, plain flags | Smoke offering and juniper confirmed as the practice (Wikipedia *Losar*, for the incense custom; the Rigpa and Mindrolling pages above for the day); no source on how it is painted and no Tibetan name read for the burner (bsang itself is given in Rabten's front matter, above). Dropped for a plain aureole. |
| Chökhor Düchen | the wheel of Dharma with two deer | Confirmed: the wheel (ཆོས་ཀྱི་འཁོར་ལོ, chos kyi 'khor lo) with deer is the Deer Park's pairing, standing on the roof of the Jokhang and on monastery roof ridges (Wikipedia *Dharmachakra*, *Jokhang*). No page names the Tibetan word for the deer. Kept. |
| Entry into the womb | the six-tusked white elephant on a cloud | The white elephant entering Māyādevī's right side is confirmed (Wikipedia *Maya*, with a white lotus in its trunk); the six tusks are not in any page read. A white elephant with a lotus, the number of tusks left out, is the motif the sources support. |
| Lhabab Düchen | the triple stair coming down through clouds; a parasol at the crown | The triple ladder with rainbows is confirmed (Wikipedia *Lhabab Duchen*); gold, silver and crystal are not named in it. The parasol is one of the eight (rin chen gdugs) but is not tied to the descent in any page. Ladder kept without the three materials; crown held back. |
| Gaden Ngamchö | rows of butter lamps; Tsongkhapa's sword and book on two lotuses | The sword and book on flower blossoms is confirmed as Tsongkhapa's painted attribute (HAR 4181, no flame named). The lamps of the festival are not in the pages read for this day. Sword and book kept, "flaming" and "two lotuses" dropped to "on flower blossoms". |
| Gutor | the year-end torma, dark wreath of fire | The torma confirmed as the offering (HAR 1062: red triangular tormas to the protectors; one use is turning back obstacles at the New Year); nothing on a wreath of fire. Held as a red triangular torma on a plain dark aureole. |

### Follow-up of 2026-11-24 (inv-h; q1353-q1356)

Read in the browser and in BDRC's full-text search; published pages only.

- **Six tusks (q1353).** Robert Beer Archive, *The Life of the Buddha*
  (tibetanart.com/artworks/273): a 2011 gouache painting by Sunlal Ratna
  Tamang of the Twelve Great Deeds of the Tibetan tradition. Mayadevi
  sleeps on her right side; in her dream a white bull elephant with six
  tusks circles her three times and enters her womb through her right
  side; he is shown above on dragon-borne clouds, the wish-granting gem
  on his silk saddle blanket. No lotus is named. The same page gives the

## The things of the nine 旧暦 festivals for their painted frames ([T6.3](#t6-3)): for each festival the motif T6.3 proposes, checked against a published description of what the festival is kept with (the NAOJ koyomi wiki's 節句 and 十三夜 pages, already sources; a Japanese encyclopaedia or a museum's catalogue of seasonal ukiyo-e for 七草, 流し雛, 鯉のぼり, 短冊, 月見団子, 栗名月, 精霊馬) and a published description of the koma-e cartouche and the round window in nishiki-e (RD409)

Read 2026-11-24 (RD409). Sources: NAOJ 暦Wiki's 節句, 中秋の名月とは, 中秋の名月とは/十三夜 and 七夕 pages (read through a Japanese Proton exit, rule 14; the pages are EUC-JP, last modified 2015-10-07, 2026-07-27, 2016-09-08 and 2026-06-07); Japanese Wikipedia (CC BY-SA, facts only, read in the headed browser on 2026-11-24) on 人日, 七草, 流し雛, 端午, 雛祭り, こいのぼり, 七夕, 短冊, 月見, お盆, 重陽, 門松, 松竹梅 and 初日の出; the British Museum's catalogue entry 1906,1220,0.723. Every verdict below is on the page's own words; none is from memory. The facts are given in our own words, the Japanese only where a word is the fact.

**One line per festival: the motif of T6.3, what the sources say, the verdict.**

- **旧正月 (kyūreki 1/1), pine, bamboo and plum, the first sunrise at the crown: confirmed in parts.** 門松 (kadomatsu), Wikipedia: pine and bamboo set before the gate at the New Year as the seat of the year-god, "the pine pledges a thousand years, the bamboo ten thousand"; in the Kansai the bamboo stands in the middle with long pine behind, and a grand one adds an old plum, nandina or bamboo-grass. 松竹梅 (shōchikubai), Wikipedia: pine, bamboo and plum together as a symbol of celebration, from the Chinese "three friends of the cold year" (歳寒三友), used on gifts and in song. 初日の出 (hatsuhinode), Wikipedia: the sunrise of 1 January, held auspicious, the first dawn of the year, visited by many. The page says the day is 1 January; the kyūreki New Year is the same custom moved by the calendar, not a source's word.
- **人日 (1/7), the seven herbs in a basket, snow on the ground: the herbs confirmed, basket and snow not found.** 人日 and 七草, Wikipedia: on the seventh day of the first month a congee of seven spring herbs is eaten (七草粥), for health through the year and to rest the stomach after the New Year feast; the custom is properly of the kyūreki 7th (the encyclopaedia says so). The seven today: seri (芹), nazuna (薺), gogyō (御形, cudweed), hakobera (繁縷, chickweed), hotokenoza (仏の座), suzuna (菘, turnip), suzushiro (蘿蔔, daikon). The Kamakura 年中行事秘抄 is the oldest text that names the kinds; in the Edo period the shogunate made the day one of the five festivals and the lords came to the castle to eat the congee; Edo also kept 薺打ち (chopping the herbs to a rhythm with seven tools) and 七草爪 (nails cut after dipping the fingers in water with the herbs). Neither page speaks of a basket or of snow; both stay the brief's own picture.
- **上巳 (3/3), peach blossom over a stream, paper dolls floating: confirmed.** 雛祭り, Wikipedia: the 3rd of the third month, the "peach festival" (桃の節句), kept at the lunar date when the peach blooms (about April), with peach blossom, diamond rice cakes, white sake; since the Meiji era on the solar date, when no peach blooms and paper or silk peach flowers stand. 3/3 was a day of purification at the riverbank (禊), which is why the shellfish sweet is shaped like an akoya shell. 流し雛, Wikipedia: the floating of dolls to send the body's defilement away on the water, the root of the doll festival, still done in places (Mochigase in Tottori, Yoshino River in Nara, Tatsuno, Iwatsuki); Genji's paper effigy put in a boat at Suma is cited as an old source; the page adds that the human-shaped dolls set on a boat date only from 1962. The doll-on-water picture is true to the custom, but the doll of the painting must be the simple paper kind, not the court dolls.
- **端午 (5/5), irises in water, carp streamers against the sky: the carp confirmed, the plant to be changed.** 端午, Wikipedia: also called 菖蒲の節句; shōbu (菖蒲) and mugwort hung from the eaves, shōbu bundles floated in the bath (菖蒲湯); shōbu shares its sound with 尚武 (honouring arms) and its leaf is like a sword, which made the day a boys' day; the court gave a medicine ball (薬玉) on this day. こいのぼり, Wikipedia: begun by the warrior class in the Edo period; the merchants' carp on a pole dates from the middle Edo, from the Chinese story of the carp that climbs the dragon gate; an Edo custom, not Kamigata; a pole with a rotating ball, a wheel (矢車), a five-coloured streamer (五色吹流し) and the carp beneath; early ones were paper; the first carp was a single black one (真鯉), the red (緋鯉) and the others came in the Meiji to Showa. The page speaks of "菖蒲", the festival plant, and does not say iris; whether the 菖蒲 of the eaves and the bath (the sweet-flag leaf) is what the painting should show, not the iris flower of the garden, is a question below.
- **七夕 (7/7), bamboo with blank strips in five colours, the Milky Way: the bamboo and strips confirmed, the five colours not; the sky corrected.** 短冊, Wikipedia: its caption "the bamboo decoration of Tanabata with strips of many colours"; in Japan today a strip is a folded-paper slip cut long and written with a wish; it began as a tag or note and a plate for poems. 七夕, Wikipedia: the Chinese "five colours" belong to the thread threaded through a needle in the 乞巧 custom and to the light seen in the heavenly river, not to the strips. NAOJ's 七夕: in the kyūreki the 7th day's moon is always the first-quarter moon, which sets late, after which the Milky Way, washed out by moonlight, appears; Wikipedia adds that a full moon hides it and that in the old calendar the half moon was seen as a boat. So the sky of the frame is a half moon low and the Milky Way, not a full moon.
- **十五夜 (8/15), the full moon at the crown, pampas grass and dumplings: confirmed.** NAOJ: the evening moon of 8/15 is the 中秋の名月, the moon-viewing coming from China in the 9th century, with feasts, poems, gardens and ponds (the moon seen in the water; the Katsura palace was designed for it), and dumplings, taro, the harvest meaning. Wikipedia 月見: offerings of pampas grass (ススキ), dumplings and the season's crops; the grass is an Edo-to-Tōhoku custom that spread over the country only after the Meiji; dumplings came into homes from the middle Edo, Edo's round, the Kyoto-Osaka ones taro-shaped, set on a low desk (三方 is not named on this page); 8/15 is also the taro moon (芋名月); the favourite places of Edo were the Sumida, the Shinobazu pond, Takanawa.
- **重陽 (9/9), chrysanthemums: confirmed.** 重陽, Wikipedia: 9/9 of the old calendar, "the chrysanthemum festival" (菊の節句) because the flower is then in bloom; chrysanthemum petals floated in wine (菊酒) from the Heian era, a cotton pad laid on the flower the night before to take up the dew for wiping the body; in China also a red-dogwood sachet and climbing a height. Basho's "my lodging at dusk, a cup of chrysanthemum wine" is cited.
- **十三夜 (9/13), the moon just short of full, chestnuts and soybeans: confirmed.** NAOJ 十三夜: a custom of Japan alone, said to begin with the moon feast of Emperor Uda on 9/13 of Engi 19 (919); the 13th-night moon is never full; named also the "later moon", the chestnut moon (栗名月) and the bean moon (豆名月). Wikipedia: as the 15th night, but with soybeans in the pod (枝豆) and chestnuts offered, hence the names (in the north of Tōhoku the names are exchanged).
- **お盆 (7/15, and 8/15 Gregorian), the welcoming fire, paper lanterns, the cucumber horse and aubergine ox: confirmed.** お盆, Wikipedia: on the evening of the 13th an open fire is lit to welcome the dead (迎え火), then offerings on the spirit shelf; floating lanterns (灯籠流し) and boat-lanterns send them off; the special 盆提灯 stand before the family altar, plain white for the first Obon after a death, patterned ones otherwise; the 精霊馬 is a cucumber (horse, so that the dead come quickly) and an aubergine (ox, so that they leave slowly and carry the offerings) with legs of hemp stalk (麻幹), matchsticks or split chopsticks; widespread in Tokyo and East Japan.

**The reference nishiki-e (for the brief, described in words, not attached).** Utagawa Hiroshige, *Suidōbashi Surugadai* (水道橋 駿河臺), no. 63 of *Meisho Edo hyakkei* (名所江戸百景), published by Uoya Eikichi, 1857 (Ansei), colour woodblock ōban; British Museum 1906,1220,0.723 (purchased from Arthur Morrison). The museum's description: carp kites flying (it counts three) over the Surugadai district, Suidō bridge at right, Edo Castle at left, Mt Fuji on the horizon; the curator notes the banners painted with Shōki (Zhong Kui) on the samurai houses. Wikipedia (こいのぼり) calls the same print the early example of a single great black carp, "the brocade carp not yet common"; the two descriptions disagree on the number of carp. The kind of picture named: a landscape colour print with a near object in strong colour against a flat sky, the print this frame's carp can be taken from. The page does not describe a round window.

**The koma-e and the round window.** Wikipedia's こま絵 (a section of 挿絵) is the small filler picture of books and magazines, with no relation to the text beside it; it is not the cartouche of a nishiki-e and says nothing of a round window. No published description of a round window (丸窓) or a round cartouche in a nishiki-e was found.

- *Composition.* A woman, half length, turned three-quarters to the left, fills the lower two thirds; she holds an open illustrated book up in one hand. The koma-e sits at the upper left, the title cartouche at the upper right; her long hairpins cross in front of the koma-e's lower edge, so that the figure stands before the inset.
- *The koma-e.* A horizontal rectangle, its upper right corner cut off at a slant, drawn round with a thin black line. Inside, a river mouth: a man working a lift net hung from a pole frame, stakes in the water, a thatched shed and bare trees on the far bank. A narrow pale-yellow label with the place's name stands against its right edge.
- *The title cartouche.* An upright red panel lettered in black, ringed by a wreath of pink and white many-petalled flowers with green leaves: a cartouche whose border is made of things.
- *Colours.* Prussian blue for the water, deep at the bottom of the inset and paling towards the shore; a band of red at the horizon under a pale sky; a flat lilac-grey ground behind the woman; her robe grey with a fine white pattern, a black collar, red underrobe, a green-and-yellow striped cloth at the lower left; the hairpins orange-red.
- *Techniques visible.* Black key-block outlines throughout; flat colour areas (the ground, the cartouche); bokashi gradations in the water and the sky of the koma-e and at the top of the ground; fine pattern blocks on the robe.

The inset of every koma-e seen is rectangular (this one, Toyokuni's *Aki no tsuki*, Kuniyoshi's other *Sankai medetai zue* sheets A-10569-5385, -5390, -5395, notched or straight-cornered). A round window remains the frame's own idea: what the print gives the brief is the inset bordered by a line with the figure overlapping it, the cartouche wreathed by the festival's things, and the blue and red bokashi of its landscape.
