## Ch. 29's unexplained words, by the other texts: a word hunt (RD161)

RD161, 2026-11-05. Sixteen questions of WB ch. 29 and the notes (q275, q277, q278, q284, q287, q288, q290, q291, q293, q296, q298, q310, q312, q320, q334, q338), each word looked at on the print first, then sought outside WB. Yigdzin-1 and MITRA were not run: every find below was read by eye on the image named, or is marked etext.

**The new witness.** The Bhutan collection (*Collected Astrological and Prognosticatory Texts from Bhutan*, Thimphu 1981, I1KG11905, printed page = image − 10; etext VEIE0OPI1829B6AF_I1KG11905) holds, from about printed p. 479, a prose digest of WB's reckoning for the dead, set in dbu med with tables: the White Beryl's Meaning, the Reckoning for the Dead Easy to Carry (བཻ་དཀར་དགོངས་དོན་གཤིན་རྩིས་འཁྱེར་བདེ, bai dkar dgongs don gshin rtsis 'khyer bde; the title as the machine reads the collection's list of contents, etext p. 10, not read on the scan). It follows WB line by line, cuts the verse to lists and marks each item off with a shad, so that it shows where WB's run-together words divide. Below it is "the digest". Its colophon, read by RD164 (reckonings.md), makes it a pupil's work of the Medical and Astrological Institute (སྨན་རྩིས་ཁང, sman rtsis khang), gathered from WB and filled out from the star reckoning, date unread, so it may be modern and it ranks below WB and Mipham. Its school was not established before that; it is a witness to WB and answers nothing where it only repeats. The second new witness is the verse manual Wish-Granting Tree of the Pebble Laying (རྒྱ་རྩིས་དགེ་ལྡན་མཁས་པའི་ཕྱག་རྒྱུན་དུ་བསྟར་བའི་རྡེལ་འགྲེམ་དཔག་བསམ་ལྗོན་ཤིང, rgya rtsis dge ldan mkhas pa'i phyag rgyun du bstar ba'i rdel 'grem dpag bsam ljon shing; etext VEIE0OPIE709B49A_I4949, 250 pages), whose verses on the dead LT repeats word for word (the same lines at I4949 etext pp. 74–93 and LT etext pp. 57–72).

### q298: the spud pa are bellows (answered)

WB prints «སྤུད་པ» (spud pa) on pp. 64–65 and 111; the digest prints the word with ba:

> ཁྱིམ་གྱི་ནུབ་བྱང་མཚམས་ན་སྒྲོ་བའམ། ཁམ་ནག་གམ། སྦུད་པ། རྐྱལ་པ་གང་རུང་ནང་། ཞྭ། སྣམ། སྤུ་འམ་དྲུ་བུ་བཅུག་པར་ཟ་འདྲེ་གབ།

(Digest, printed pp. 494–495, img. 504–505, by eye. At the north-west of the house, in a basket, a black *kham* (ཁམ, kham, a vessel not identified), a bellows or a leather bag, with a hat, cloth, fur or thread put in, the eating demon hides.) This is WB's «ནག་གམ་སྤུད་པ་རྒད་པོ་འམ། །རྐྱལ་གསུམ་ནང་ན་མགོ་འམ་ཞྭ།» ("black, or an old *spud pa*, or inside the three bags, a head or a hat").

The bellows (སྦུད་པ, sbud pa) is a blower of goat or other hide for the fire (Tshig mdzod chen mo, Rangjung Yeshe, Valby, Dag tshig gsar bsgrigs). It fits every place WB has the word: an old one, a worn one («སྤུད་གོག», spud gog, beside the worn bag «རྐྱལ་གོག»; a worn bellows, «སྦུད་གོག», is something a pot is set on in a Shangpa Kagyu story, BDRC snippet, VE3JT13356_006), and the rite of p. 111, whose nine and nine in the four directions have their mouths turned outward and are blown (read again on the reprint, I3CN5029 img. 117, l. 15):

> ཕྱོགས་བཞིར་སྤུད་པ་དགུ་དགུ་ནི། །ཁ་ཕྱིར་བསྟན་ལ་འབུད་པ་དང་།

(In the four directions nine and nine bellows, mouths turned outward, blown.) So «རྒྱ་ཡི་སྤུད་པ་དགུ་གདུག» (rgya yi spud pa dgu gdug) is the rite of the nine bellows of China. Other prints of the title: the 1996 etext reads the notes' «སྦུད་པ་དགུ» at p. 517 and again at the notes' index (etext p. 552; machine), and a manual in the Khams collection (MK's series, I2PD18783, search only, snippet) «ཁ་བཅད་རྒྱ་ཡི་སྦུད་པ་དགུ་སྡུག་གཏོ». On the Gser thang reprint (img. 523, p. 517, l. 9, 2.5×) the stack is ས over a lower letter not told as པ or བ.

The notes' second word stands: «སྤུད་པ་དགུ་རྒྱུག་དྲུག་ནི་གཤེད་བཞི་ཟིན་ཤི་དང་། དུར་ཞུགས་དང་…» (reprint, by eye for «རྒྱུག་དྲུག»), "the nine bellows' six *rgyug* are: the deaths on the four executioners, the entry to the grave …". The six are the occasions of the rite, as p. 111's list of occasions suggests; what «རྒྱུག» (rgyug) names is not stated. The 1996 etext's «རྒྱུ་བཞི» is the OCR's.

### q334: «ལྷང» is «ལྒང་ཕུག», a bladder (answered)

P. 106, l. 4 is «སྙིང་སྣ་ར་ཡི་ལྒང་ཕུག་བཅུག» in the 1996 etext (BDRC's text of the print, etext p. 114), and the stack on the reprint (img. 112, 2×) is blotted but agrees with a head letter over ga, with no ha below. The bladder (ལྒང་ཕུག, lgang phug; Tshig mdzod chen mo: the urine vessel, and a blown bladder; Valby, Ives Waldo) is common in ritual and medical writing (BDRC, 190 hits). So: "the heart-and-nose [offering] put into a goat's bladder". The Zhol print has a shorter line at this place, «སྙིང་སྣ་རོ་ཡི་ཁ་ནང་གཞུག» (img. 783, 5×), "the heart-and-nose put into the corpse's mouth", with no goat's bladder, and the digest agrees with it («སྙིང་སྣ་རོ་ཁར་འཇུག», printed p. 541, img. 551, etext).

### q338: «ཕ་གུ» stands for «ཕ་ཁུ» (answered)

WB's own ch. 24 has the formula (vol. 1, p. 388, img. 398, l. 8, by eye):

> སྤྱིར་ནི་དེ་དག་ཐམས་ཅད་ལ། །ཤིང་གིས་ཕ་ཁུ་མེས་གསུམ་བརྩི། །མེ་ཡིས་བུ་ཚ་མ་སྨད་བརྩི། །ས་ཡིས་མ་ཕྱི་ཡུལ་…

(In general, in all of these: by wood reckon the three, father, uncle and grandfather; by fire sons, grandchildren, mother and lower kin; by earth grandmother and home …) Zhe chen rgyal tshab repeats it (ZG's volume I4PD2444, etext p. 310, machine: «ཤིང་གིས་ཕ་ཁུ་མེས་གསུམ»), and BDRC has 24 hits of «ཕ་ཁུ་མེས་གསུམ» (pha khu mes gsum), LT and UT among them; «ཕ་གུ་མེས» has none. So p. 135's «ཕ་གུ་མེས་གསུམ», carved so on the blocks and the reprint, is a spelling of the three, father, paternal uncle and grandfather.

### q293: «རྨང་པོ» is the line's failure (answered)

To the claim's headword and idiom, two manuals of reckoning add sentences that fix the sense (etext, not read on the scan): LT p. 128 (etext p. 136) «རྨང་པོ་ལ་བུ་གསུམ་སྐྱེ», three sons are born to the *rmang po*, among the blessings; LT p. 163 (etext p. 171) «བུ་བརྒྱ་ཡོད་ཀྱང་རྨང་པོར་འགྲོ», even with a hundred sons he goes *rmang po*; UT p. 99 (etext p. 121) «གཤིན་རྩིས་དབུལ་དང་རྨང་པོ་འོང», in the reckoning for the dead come poverty and *rmang po*. So the childless man (རྨང་པོ, rmang po) of Dan Martin and the Brda dkrol stands, and p. 104's fourteenth place brings childlessness, beside the fifteenth's poverty.

### q291: the btsad po is a lord, among ranks (answered)

The digest lists him among the corpses not to be hidden (printed p. 556, img. 566, by eye):

> སྦ་རུ་མི་རུང་རོ་རིགས་ནི། །བཙད་པོ་དགེ་བཤེས་སྔགས་པའི་རོ།

(The kinds of corpse that may not be hidden: the corpses of the lord, the spiritual friend and the mantra-holder.) The verse manual has «བརྒྱད་དཀར་ཡུགས་ས་དགུ་དམར་སྟེང་། །བཙད་པོ་རྒན་ཁོག་རོ་མི་གཏོང་།» (I4949 etext p. 93, LT etext p. 72, machine): on the eight-white a mourning, on the nine-red the lord (བཙད་པོ, btsad po) and the worn old are not sent. With the old-word gloss of the dictionaries (claim *btsad-po-old-word-for-lord*), the lord is a rank of the living world, not one spirit.

### q277: «འཆོལ་བ», irregular (answered)

The Zhol blocks carve the same letter in both places, the count line (img. 723, last line, 8×) and the heading (img. 725, l. 3, 9×): two-lobed with the vowel o and no tick at the top right, read «འཆོལ་བ་དྲུག». The search-only copy of WB in the medical collection (MW2PD17386; etext I1KG81102) has «འདུར་ཐང་འཆོལ་བ་དྲུག» too, and Gyurme Dorje, in *Tibetan Elemental Divination Paintings* (2001, search only, its English seen in BDRC's snippet), renders «'dur-thang 'chol-ba drug» as the six irregular modes of funeral status. So the disordered shares (འཆོལ་བ, 'chol ba) it is; the 1996 line's «འཚོལ་བ» on p. 68 is that print's slip.

### q284 and q296: the mansions

The digest heads the twelfth entry of the mansions with «དབོ» (dbo) in its boxed heading and again in the remedy (printed p. 503, img. 513, by eye):

> དབོ། གཟའ་བདུད་ཀྱིས་ཁྱེར། ཕ་མེས་རུས་པའི་དུར་རྙིང་གིས་གནོད། … དབོ་ལ་འབྲུ་སྣའི་ཟན་གླུད་ནི། །ཁྲག་གིས་དམར་པོ་བྱས་ནས་ཀྱང་།

(*Dbo*: the planet-demon carries off; the old grave of the fathers' clan harms. … For *dbo*, a dough ransom of grains, reddened with blood.) This is WB's «སྦོ་ལ་ཤི་ན་གཟའ་བདུད་ཁྱེར། །ཕ་མེས་རུས་པའི་དུར་རྙིང་…»: q284 is answered, the stack is U. Phalgunī (དབོ, dbo), as *sbo*. WB2's etext has this page too garbled to find the line, and its 1210-px scan was not read.

The digest repeats p. 122 by year (printed p. 514, img. 524, by eye): «འབྲུག སྦོ་ལ་གསོན་པོར་ཆགས་ཆེ་མང་།» (dragon: on *sbo*) and «སྦྲུལ སྦོ་སྨད་ལ་གསོན་པོར་ཆགས་ཆེ།» (snake: on *sbo smad*), then «རྟ མཆུ་འམ་གྲེར།» (horse: on *mchu* or *gre*) and «ལུག བྱི་བཞིན་ལ» (sheep: on *byi bzhin*). So the 1996 print's «སྦ་སྨད» is «སྦོ་སྨད» (sbo smad), as the blocks carve it. Lag sor (ལག་སོར) has a second witness as Anurādhā: «བཅུ་དྲུག་ལྷ་མཚམས་ལག་སོར» in the Bhutan collection's list of mansions with their second names (etext p. 116, machine). Which mansion *sbo smad* is stays open: that list has no such second name («བཅུ་གཅིག་དབོ་དང་ཁྲ»), and the only other «སྦོ་སྟོད» and «སྦོ་སྨད» found are the parts of the sky dog (UT etext p. 257, Bhutan collection etext pp. 228, 242: «མཇིང་པ་སྦོ་སྨད», neck, lower belly), which is a different use.

### q312: the provisions, divided by the digest (mostly answered)

The digest gives the provisions of each trigram (སྤར་ཁ, spar kha) as a list (by eye), for khon (ཁོན), dwa (དྭ), zin (ཟིན) and zon (ཟོན):

| Trigram | Digest | Page, image |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *khon* | «ལྕང་མ། བྱ་ཐི་བ་དང་། ཟར་མ། ཕུ་ཐུང་། དྲོད་སྨན། སོག་པ། སྲང་བཅས་དུར་སར་རྫོངས།» | 533, 543 |

| *zin* | «སྟག་ཤིང་དང་། བྱ་ནེ་ཙོ། སོ་བ། སོར་ལེ། སྟག་ལྤགས། བོང་བུའི་སྐྱུར་རྩིབ། སྐ་རགས་བཅས་དུར་སར་རྫོངས།» | 541–542, 551–552 |

(Khon: willow, the pigeon, sesame, a sleeved garment, warm medicine, *sog pa*, *srang*. Dwa: the *tshar* tree, the *'jol mo* bird's feather, *ju rtse*, goat and donkey, clothes and armour, the left sour rib. Zin: the birch, the parrot, coarse barley, *sor le*, tiger skin, the donkey's sour rib, a belt. Zon: the *gsom* tree, the hoopoe, *phug*, silk, the flesh of a male yak-cross (མཛོ་གླང, mdzo glang): each sent to the grave.) The other trigrams, li (ལི), khen (ཁེན), kham (ཁམ) and gin (གིན) (etext, machine): *li* «སེ་ཤིང་དང་སྐྱ་ཀའི་སྒྲོ། འབྲས་གཡུ། ཞྭ། ཕྱིང་པ། མགོ་ཤ། མར་ཁུ», *khen* «སྦྱར་པ་དང་། བྱ་ཁྲུང་ཁྲུང་། བྲ་བོ། ཁྱི་ཤ», *kham* «སྤེན་ཤིང་དང་། བྱ་ཁ་ཏ། སྲན་མ། བྲ་བོ», *gin* «ཤུག་པ་དང་། བྱ་འུག་པ། ཟ་འོག །གྲོ». Each list runs tree, bird, grain, then clothes and flesh.

From this:

- «སོག་པ་སྲང» is two items, *sog pa* and *srang* (སྲང, an ounce of money, as the recitation's «ཕུག་སྲང་སོག་པ» as the old woman's wealth would have it); «ཕུག་དར» is two items, *phug* and silk; «སོ་བ་སོར་ལ» is the coarse barley and *sor le*; «སྟག་ལྤགས་བོང། །སྐྱུར་རྩིབ» is tiger skin and the donkey's rib; WB's «དང་ཁྲག» at *dwa* is «ཁྲབ», armour, in the digest.
- «ཇུ་ཚེར» is «ཇུ་ཚེ» or «ཇུ་རྩེ» and the next word «ར་བོང» (the 1996 etext has «ཇུ་ཚེ་ར་བོང་གོས»): the grain ju rtse (ཇུ་རྩེ, ju rtse). ### q287: the third examination's objects

- **«སླ་ང་རྩེས་བྱིན་བརྒྱ་བཞི» (p. 59): answered.** The digest's cell for the ox and bird (printed p. 488, img. 498, by eye): «སླ་ངར་གཤེད་གབ། བན་བོན་ལྔས་གླང་ངམ། ལུག་གསོབ་སྟེང་དུ་གཤེད་གསོད། ཟླ་བཞིའི་མཚམས་ལྡོག་ངོ་། སླ་ང་རྩིས་མཁན་ལ་སྦྱིན། བརྒྱ་བཞི་བྱ།» (The executioner hides in a pan. ### q440 and q447: the first portion is the mash's, and the cloth covers the beer (answered)

WB's «སྦང་མ་ཕུད་གཏོར་ཡོལ་བས་སྒྲིབ» (p. 224; the reprint, the Zhol and the Taikhang prints read «སྦང་མ», claim *wb-sbang-ma-is-the-beer-mash*) is a line of the rat day. SY (printed p. 345; etext):

> བྱི་ཉིར་མོ་འདེབས་… བཟང་། ཆང་རྫར་གནས། བྱིས་པར་ངན། སྦང་མའི་ཕུད་གཏོང་ཡོལ་བ་བཏང་བས་ཉེས་པ་ཞི། བལ་སྐུད་བྱི་སྐེར་བཏགས་པས་ཐུབ།

(Rat day: [its works, left out here]; it stays at the beer jar; bad for children. By giving the first portion of the mash and putting out a screen the fault is quieted; a wool thread tied at the *byi sker* overcomes. In SY the seat and its cure follow the day's list of works, so the line is the rat day's, as WB's is.) The Drigung verse work has the same day in verse (etext p. 58):

> བྱི་བའི་ཉི་མར་ཆང་རྫས་ཕྱར་བ་བཀབ། །བལ་གྱི་སྐུད་པ་ས་ཁྱི་སྐོར་བཏགས་ཐུབ། །

(On the rat day cover the beer things with a cloth [ཕྱར་བ, *phyar ba*]; a wool thread tied round the earth-dog overcomes.)

What this settles. The genitive in «སྦང་མའི་ཕུད» makes the first portion the mash's: the beer's dregs (སྦང་མ, *sbang ma*), not a bee's honey (q447: answered; the mash it is, no misprint). The mash is scattered in other texts too, as feed: in the story of the envoy Gar's tests the dregs are strewn on a plain and the birds that eat in pairs show which are mother and young («མགར་གྱིས་ཐང་ཡངས་ཅིག་ལ། སྦང་མ་གཏོར་ནས་བཞག་སྟེ། དེར་བྱ་རྣམས་ཁ་གཏད་དོ», Sakya Sonam Gyaltsen's *Mirror of Royal Genealogies* and other tellings, BDRC 18 hits of «སྦང་མ་གཏོར», etext, not read on a scan). The cloth (ཡོལ་བ, *yol ba*) is "put out" (བཏང་, *btang*) in SY, which is the verb of spreading a screen (a Drigung history: the camp was hidden in fog, «ཡོལ་བ་བཏང་པས་སོག་པོས་མ་མཐོང་», "a screen was put out and the Mongols did not see", BDRC snippet), and the verse work says what is covered: the beer things (ཆང་རྫས, *chang rdzas*) with a covering cloth (ཕྱར་བ; Tshig mdzod chen mo and Ives Waldo: a cover of wool or hide for grain and the like). So in these two almanacs the cloth covers the beer and its vessel, not the threshold (q440: answered, as far as one almanac reading of the line goes: the jar and what is brewed in it). The harm falls on children («བྱིས་པར་ངན», SY), as WB's «བྱིས་པ་དག་ལ་ངན» has it; a screen between the jar and the children fits both, and neither text says so.

### q439 and q446: the almanacs drop «ཡོ་ནས་འདྲེན» (narrowed, not answered)

SY and the Drigung verse work give the rat day a different last remedy: a wool thread (བལ་སྐུད, *bal skud*) tied, at the *byi sker* (SY) or round the *sa khyi* (the verse work). Neither has «ཡོ», «ཁ་ཡོ» or «འདྲེན» in the rat day's lines, and the other twelve-day lines of both are quite unlike WB's (the dog day: «ཡ་ཐེམ་མ་ཐེམ་གནས། ཐེམ་བར་རོ་ཐུག་ངན། སྒྲིབ་ན་ཁྱི་གཅིག་ལ་སྤང་ཟན་བྱིན། གཅིག་ལ་མི་ཁའི་རྫས་བཀལ་ལ་གར་འགྲོའི་ཕྱོགས་སུ་ཁ་བསྟན་ན་ཐུབ།» in SY; «ཁྱི་ཉི་སྒྲིབ་ན་ཁྱི་གཅིག་སྦང་ཟན་བྱིན། །ཡང་ཁྱི་གཅིག་ལ་མི་ཁའི་རྫས་བཀལ་ནས། །གར་སོང་ཕྱོགས་སུ་སྔོན་དུ་ཕྱིན་པས་ཐུབ།» in the verse work: threshold-stay, a corpse set on the threshold is bad; if afflicted give one dog the mash-cake, load another with something of the person's mouth and turn its face the way it goes). Two things follow. First, WB's «ཡོ་ནས་འདྲེན» is not borrowed by these writers: they replace it with remedies of their own, so they do not gloss it. Second, the almanacs' remedies are acts done with animals and cloth in the household, which fits «འདྲེན» as a verb of leading away (the dog is led by its going) but does not show what «ཡོ» is. The earlier hunt's result stands: «ཡོ» alone is "crooked, awry" or "all" in the dictionaries, «ཁ་ཡོ» "mouth awry" in one (Valby); «ཡོ་ནས», «ཁ་ཡོ་ནས», «ཆང་རྫ་ཁ་ཡོ», «རྫ་ཁ་ཡོ», «ཡོ་ནས་ཆང» have no hit in BDRC beyond the WB copies and the Bhutan table already counted. The only new ones are «ཡོ་ཆང» (13 hits, a Bon text of offerings, «ན་ནིང་བཙོས་པའི་ཡོ་ཆང», read as old beer, and noise; Bon, not a source) and «ཆང་འདྲེན་ནོ» (2, "serves beer" in the life of Tsangnyön Heruka and a Tibetan-medicine anthology, a woman serving beer in a skull cup). So «འདྲེན» of a drink is "to serve", attested for beer in a plain sense; that is all this hunt adds to the open question 446.

### q436: the 9th's result reads «ན་ཆུང་འགྲོགས» elsewhere (narrowed)

The Drigung verse work has the same list of thirty results for cutting a child's hair, in a section headed "how to reckon the cutting of the hair of children and others" (etext p. 14; the Khams copy has the same lines, search snippet):

> ཚེས་གཅིག་ཚེ་ཐུང་གཉིས་ལ་ཁ་མཆུ་འོང་། །གསུམ་བཟང་བཞི་ཕྱུག་ལྔ་ལ་དབང་ཐང་ཆེ། །དྲུག་ལ་ཁ་རྗེ་ཆེ་ཞིང་དབང་ཐང་དར། །བདུན་ལ་ཞལ་ལྕེ་འོང་ཞིང་བརྒྱད་ཚེ་རིང་། །དགུ་ལ་ན་ཆུང་འགྲོགས་དང་ཚེས་བཅུ་ལ། །དབང་ཐང་ཆེ་ཞིང་བཅུ་གཅིག་དབང་པོ་རྣོ། །བཅུ་གཉིས་སྲོག་ཟིན་བཅུ་གསུམ་ཤིན་ཏུ་བཟང་། །བཅུ་བཞི་བཅོ་ལྔ་དག་ལ་ཕྱུག་པོ་འོང་། །བཅུ་དྲུག་བཅུ་བདུན་མདོག་སྔོ་བཅོ་བརྒྱད་ལ། །ཚེ་རིང་བཅུ་དགུར་བཟང་ཞིང་ཉི་ཤུ་ལ། །སྟོབས་བསྐྱེད་ཉེར་གཅིག་ཚེ་རིང་ཉེར་གཉིས་སུ། །ཟས་རྙེད་ཉེར་གསུམ་ཉེར་བཞི་ཉེར་ལྔ་ལ། །ནད་མང་ཉེར་དྲུག་ཉེར་བདུན་བདེ་སྐྱིད་ལྡན། །ཉེར་བརྒྱད་འཐབ་མོ་འོང་ཉན་ཉེར་དགུ་ལ། །ཉེར་འཚེ་མང་ཞིང་གནམ་སྟོང་ལྷག་ཏུ་འཛེམ།

(The 1st short life, the 2nd quarrels; the 3rd good, the 4th wealth, the 5th great power; the 6th «ཁ་རྗེ་ཆེ» and power flourishes; the 7th a dispute, the 8th long life; the 9th company of the young; the 10th great power, the 11th sharp faculties; the 12th «སྲོག་ཟིན» (the life seized), the 13th very good; the 14th and 15th wealth; the 16th to 18th colour «མདོག་སྔོ» (blue) at the 18th; the 19th good and long life; the 21st strength grows, the 22nd long life; the 23rd food found; the 26th many illnesses, the 27th ease; the 28th a fight, the 29th much harm, and the empty day to be avoided. The lines for the 16th–18th, 20th and 24th–25th are left unglossed.) The list is WB's (p. 404's verse, hair-dates.md) with different results at the 3rd, 4th, 5th, 6th, 7th and 12th, and the same at the 1st, 2nd, 8th, 10th and 11th; the 9th is the case. The result is «ན་ཆུང་འགྲོགས» (na chung 'grogs), "company with the young", where WB has «ན་ཆུང་མོད་འགྱུར». The young (ན་ཆུང, *na chung*) are young women and youths (Ives Waldo: teenager, young girl; Tshig gter rgya mtsho: a young woman or a young man), and the verb is "to keep company". Placed between the 8th's long life and the 10th's great power, the line is a neutral-to-good one in this list, as FPMT's "meeting youthful people" is in the haircut tables of hair-dates.md. The verse does not call it a warning. Whether WB's «མོད་འགྱུར» (the dictionaries have no such compound) is this verb's synonym stays open; the answer to the question, gain or warning, is that the one parallel the sources give reads it as company, with no mark of harm. The same words «ན་ཆུང་འགྲོགས» stand in a verse of the poem *Dpag bsam 'khri shing* and its commentaries, «བུང་བའི་ན་ཆུང་འགྲོགས་པ་ཡི», the company of the young bees (BDRC, ten hits): not about hair.

### q432: the sun's klung (not advanced)

No text outside WB, KD and the lists already counted has «ཉི་མའི་ཀླུང»: BDRC finds «ཉི་མའི་ཀླུང་དང་» only in WB's own copies (1 hit), and the other hits of «ཉི་མའི་ཀླུང» are WB again and the Bon canon's «གནམ་ལ་ཉི་མའི་ཀླུང་བཟང་» ("in the sky the sun's klung is good"; Bon, not a source). KD's peahen verse (etext, claim *wb-langging-sun-klung*) stays the one parallel, «ཉི་མའི་ཀླུང་རྟས་»: the sun's klung-rta (luck-wind) held by the bird by the breath taken. «ཟླ་བའི་ཀླུང» (22 hits) is the moon's counterpart in the year headings of almanacs («འབྱུང་བ་བཞི་ལྡན། ཟླ་བའི་ཀླུང་། བསྐོར་སྨེ་དགུ་དམར…», a Drigung and a collected-works almanac, snippets): a year is named by its moon's klung as by its element and mewa. That is consistent with the reading of RD65 (the klung of ch. 13's column is a listed property of the year) and adds no gloss.
